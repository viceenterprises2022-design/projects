Satellites are objects that move around a larger object due to gravity.
There are two main types of satellites:
1. Natural Satellites
These are naturally occurring objects in space that orbit planets.
Example:
Earth’s Moon
Moons of Jupiter and Saturn
The Moon is a natural satellite of Earth.
2. Artificial Satellites
These are human-made objects launched into space.
They orbit Earth or other planets for different purposes.
Uses:
Communication
Weather forecasting
GPS navigation
Scientific research
Television broadcasting
Examples:
International Space Station
Weather satellites
GPS satellites
Types of Artificial Satellites by Purpose
Simple Definition
A satellite is an object that revolves around another object in space because of gravity.
Example:
Moon around Earth
Earth around the Sun
Artificial satellites around Earth
Geostationary Satellite
A geostationary satellite is a satellite that:
moves around Earth,
travels in the same direction as Earth’s rotation,
and takes exactly 24 hours to complete one orbit.
Because of this, it appears to stay fixed above the same place on Earth.
Important Features
A geostationary satellite:
orbits above the equator,
stays at a very high altitude,
appears motionless from Earth.
Its approximate height is:
above Earth’s surface.
Why is it useful?
Since the satellite remains above the same location, ground antennas do not need to keep moving to track it.
Uses:
Television broadcasting
Weather monitoring
Communication
Internet services
Examples
Examples include:
communication satellites,
weather satellites,
broadcasting satellites.

Exploring the Universe and Celestial Bodies
Detailed Notes with Importance and Real-World Relevance
Why Are We Learning This?
Humans have always looked at the sky and wondered:
• Where did the universe come from?
• Why do planets move?
• What are stars made of?
• Could life exist elsewhere?
Learning astronomy helps us:
• Understand our place in the universe
• Develop scientific thinking and observation skills
• Explain natural phenomena like eclipses and seasons
• Understand technologies such as satellites, GPS, and communication
• Appreciate how scientific models change with evidence
Astronomy connects physics, chemistry, geography, mathematics, and technology into one fascinating field.
1. Big Bang Theory
The Big Bang Theory explains the origin of the universe.
Main Ideas:
• The universe began around 13.8 billion years ago.
• It started from an extremely hot and dense state.
• The universe has been expanding ever since.
Important Evidence:
• Expansion of galaxies
• Cosmic microwave background radiation
• Presence of hydrogen and helium in space
Why Are We Learning This?
• Helps us understand the origin of matter, stars, and galaxies
• Demonstrates how scientific theories are built using evidence
• Encourages curiosity about the universe and existence
2. Timeline of the Universe
0 seconds – Big Bang occurs
Few minutes – First atomic nuclei form
380,000 years – Atoms form and light travels freely
Millions of years – First stars and galaxies form
4.6 billion years ago – Solar system forms
Present day – Expanding universe
Why Are We Learning This?
• Helps students visualize cosmic evolution
• Introduces the enormous timescales involved in astronomy
• Builds understanding of how Earth formed within the universe
3. Solar System Models
Geocentric Model:
• Earth at the center
• Supported by Ptolemy
Heliocentric Model:
• Sun at the center
• Proposed by Nicolaus Copernicus
Why Are We Learning This?
• Shows how scientific understanding changes with evidence
• Demonstrates the importance of observation and mathematics
• Encourages evidence-based thinking instead of assumptions
4. Solar System
The solar system includes:
• The Sun
• Eight planets
• Dwarf planets
• Moons
• Asteroids
• Comets
Inner Planets:
Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars
Outer Planets:
Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune
Asteroid Belt:
Located between Mars and Jupiter.
Why Are We Learning This?
• Helps us understand planetary motion and gravity
• Explains day/night and seasons
• Helps students understand Earth’s position in space
5. Galaxies
A galaxy is a huge collection of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter held together by gravity.
Types of Galaxies:
• Spiral galaxies
• Elliptical galaxies
• Irregular galaxies
The Milky Way is our home galaxy.
Why Are We Learning This?
• Helps students understand the vastness of the universe
• Introduces the scale of cosmic structures
• Builds perspective about Earth’s small place in the cosmos
6. Eclipses
Solar Eclipse:
• Moon blocks sunlight reaching Earth
Lunar Eclipse:
• Earth blocks sunlight reaching the Moon
Why Are We Learning This?
• Helps explain shadows and orbital motion
• Demonstrates predictable celestial motion
• Shows how science explains natural events once surrounded by superstition
7. Life Cycle of Stars
Stages:
1. Nebula
2. Protostar
3. Main Sequence Star
4. Red Giant or Supergiant
5. White Dwarf / Neutron Star / Black Hole
Why Are We Learning This?
• Helps students understand stellar evolution
• Explains how heavy elements are formed
• Connects astronomy with chemistry and physics
Interesting Idea:
The atoms in our body were once formed inside stars.
8. Celestial Bodies
Comets:

Meteors:

Why Are We Learning This?
• Helps us understand different space objects
• Explains space impacts and planetary defense
• Connects space science to Earth’s history
9. Satellites and Their Types
Natural Satellites:
• Naturally occurring moons
Artificial Satellites:
• Human-made satellites
Uses:
• Communication
• GPS

Why Are We Learning This?
• Connects astronomy to daily life
• Helps students understand modern technologies
• Demonstrates the importance of space science in society
Final Understanding
By learning astronomy and celestial bodies, students develop:
• Scientific curiosity
• Logical reasoning
• Understanding of scale and time
• Appreciation for evidence-based thinking
Most importantly, students begin asking:
“How does the universe work, and what is our place within it?”

Designing an investigation (Criterion B)
🔸Research Question (RQ)
Research Question:
How does changing the force applied to the plunger of Syringe A affect the movement of the plunger in Syringe B when the two syringes are connected with water?
🔸 Variables
a) Independent Variable
The force applied on the plunger of Syringe A.
b) Dependent Variable
The distance moved by the plunger in Syringe B.
c) Controlled Variables
Size and type of syringes (both identical).
No air bubbles in the tubing.
Height/position of the syringes.
Same tubing length and material.
Temperature of the water (so viscosity stays the same).
🔸 Hypothesis
If the force applied to the plunger of Syringe A increases, then the plunger in Syringe B will also move more, because pressure applied to a liquid is transmitted equally in all directions (Pascal’s Law).
🔸 Justification of the Hypothesis
When I push Syringe A, I increase the pressure inside the water.
According to Pascal’s Law, this pressure is passed equally through all parts of the liquid, so Syringe B experiences the same pressure change. Because the syringes are the same size, the pressure causes almost the same plunger movement. Therefore, it makes sense to predict that more force on Syringe A will produce more movement in Syringe B.
🔸 Experimental Design
Description of Setup
Two identical syringes are connected with a plastic tube and completely filled with water. The plungers can move freely, and no air bubbles are present.
How I Change the Independent Variable
I push the plunger of Syringe A with different amounts of force (light push, medium push, firm push).
How I Measure the Dependent Variable
I measure how many centimetres the plunger in Syringe B moves using a ruler.
How I Control Other Variables
I keep the syringes the same size.
I make sure the system stays full of water.
I keep the tubing length the same each time.
I test at the same table height.
I repeat each trial and take the average to improve accuracy.

Cr. D practice:
Hydraulic systems that operate based on Pascal’s Law are widely used in both industrial settings and public transport. In automobile service stations, construction sites, and waste management facilities, hydraulic lifts and compactors allow workers to move or lift heavy loads using relatively small forces. These systems reduce physical strain, increase efficiency, and make many jobs safer and faster.
Hydraulic technology is also essential in public transport. Many buses, trains, and metro systems use hydraulic braking systems that allow large, heavy vehicles to slow down or stop safely while carrying many passengers. Reliable braking systems improve road safety, protect pedestrians, and increase public trust in public transport. This can encourage people to use shared transport instead of private vehicles, helping to reduce traffic congestion and air pollution.
Despite these benefits, hydraulic systems rely on pressurised fluids that must be carefully maintained. Leaks of hydraulic fluid can contaminate soil and water, causing environmental damage. Poor maintenance or system failure can lead to serious accidents, especially in public transport, where many lives may be at risk. These risks raise ethical concerns about worker and passenger safety and highlight the responsibility of companies to maintain equipment properly.
As hydraulic systems become more common in industry and transport, governments play an important role in setting safety standards, enforcing regular inspections, and ensuring proper disposal of hydraulic fluids. Effective laws and regulations help maximise the benefits of hydraulic technology while reducing risks to people and the environment.

A cyclist is training for a race and decides to record her journey using a GPS tracker. The distance-time graph of her 40-minute ride is provided below.
[Draw a distance-time graph on the board showing:
0-10 minutes: Steady increase in distance
10-15 minutes: Flat line (no change in distance)
15-30 minutes: Steeper increase in distance
30-40 minutes: Gradual increase in distance
Interpret the data a) Describe what the cyclist was doing during the 10-15 minute period. b) During which time interval was the cyclist moving the fastest? Justify your answer using the graph.
a) Calculate the average speed of the entire journey. b) Explain the difference between average speed and instantaneous speed.
a) Determine the instantaneous speed of the cyclist at the 20-minute mark. Show your method. b) Compare the instantaneous speed at 20 minutes to the average speed of the entire journey. Explain any differences.
0–10 min: steady slope = 0.2 km/min (→ 12 km/h)
10–15 min: flat (no change in distance) = stopped/pausing
15–30 min: steeper slope = 0.4 km/min (→ 24 km/h)
30–40 min: gradual slope = 0.15 km/min (→ 9 km/h)
These produce a total distance of 9.50 km over 40 min.
Interpretations from the graph
a) What was the cyclist doing during 10–15 minutes?
She was stopped or paused — the distance stays constant (flat line), meaning no motion / zero speed during that interval (could be resting, waiting at traffic lights, adjusting equipment, etc.).
b) When was the cyclist moving fastest? Justify.
15–30 minutes. The graph is steepest there — slope (rise/run) is largest, meaning the highest speed. Numerically I used 0.4 km/min = 24 km/h for that segment.
Average speed — whole journey
We compute step-by-step.
Total distance = 9.50 km.
Total time = 40 minutes = 40 / 60 = 2/3 hours.
Average speed = total distance / total time
= 9.50 km ÷ (2/3 h)
= 9.50 × (3/2) km/h
= 14.25 km/h.
In m/s: 14.25 × 1000 / 3600 = 3.958... m/s (about 3.96 m/s).
Average speed vs instantaneous speed
Average speed = total distance / total time (summary over whole trip).
Instantaneous speed = speed at a single moment (slope of the d–t curve at that time; mathematically the derivative).
Average tells you the overall pace including stops/slow sections. Instantaneous tells you how fast she was going right then.
Instantaneous speed at 20 minutes
20 min lies in 15–30 min linear segment (constant slope). For that segment slope = 0.4 km per minute.
Compute:
0.4 km/min × 60 = 24 km/h instantaneous speed at 20 min.
In m/s: 24 × 1000 / 3600 = 6.666... m/s (≈ 6.67 m/s).
Method shown: instantaneous speed = slope = Δdistance / Δtime for the tangent (or segment, since segment is linear). Because the 15–30 min segment is linear, the tangent slope = segment slope = 0.4 km/min.
Comparison: instantaneous at 20 min vs average
Instantaneous at 20 min = 24.0 km/h.
Average speed (whole trip) = 14.25 km/h.
Why the difference? Because the average includes the 10–15 min stop and slower parts (0–10 and 30–40 have lower speeds). Instantaneous at 20 min is only the speed during her fastest stretch, so it is higher than the overall average.

Free-body diagrams are diagrams used to show the relative magnitude and direction of all forces acting upon an object in a given situation. A free-body diagram is an example of the vector diagrams where the size of the arrow reflects the magnitude of the force and the direction of the arrow shows the direction that the force is acting. Summary: Graphical Representation of Motion
1. Importance of Graphs
Graphs provide a clear visual method to present data. In Physics, they show relationships between quantities such as distance vs. time or velocity vs. time. They help analyze uniform, non-uniform, accelerated, or rest motion. To describe the motion of an object, we can use line graphs. In this case, line graphs show dependence of one physical quantity, such as distance or velocity, on another quantity, such as time.
2. Distance–Time Graphs
- Axes: Time → x-axis; Distance → y-axis.
We know that when an object travels equal distances in equal intervals of time, it moves
with uniform speed. This shows that the distance travelled by the object is directly
proportional to time taken. Thus, for uniform speed, a graph of distance travelled against time is a straight line.
Speed = slope of line = (s2 - s1) / (t2 - t1).
Note that, you can also use the term uniform velocity in place of uniform speed if you take the magnitude of displacement equal to the distance travelled by the object along the y-axis.
- Non-uniform Motion: Graph is curved (non-linear).
3. Velocity–Time Graphs
- Axes: Time → x-axis; Velocity → y-axis.
- Uniform Velocity: Straight horizontal line. Displacement = area under graph.
- Uniform Acceleration: Straight sloping line. Displacement = rectangle + triangle area.
- Non-uniform Acceleration: Graph can be curved or irregular.
4. Key Examples and Activities
- Uniform Acceleration: Car velocity increases equally every 5s → straight sloping line.
- Distance from Graphs: Area under v–t graph = displacement. Rectangle → uniform velocity; Rectangle + triangle → uniform acceleration.
- Non-uniform Acceleration: Decreasing velocity (sloping down) or irregular variation.
-Activity:
5. Essential Takeaways
1. Distance–time graphs: straight line = uniform, curve = non-uniform.
2. Slope of distance–time graph = speed.
3. Velocity–time graphs: horizontal = constant velocity, sloping = uniform acceleration, curve = non-uniform acceleration.
4. Area under velocity–time graph = displacement.
5. Graphs make motion data easier to analyze than tables.

Conversions in Physics: Changing from One Unit to Another
🌟 What Are Conversions?
In physics, we often measure things like length, mass, time, and temperature. But what if someone gives you the length in centimeters, and you need it in meters? That’s when conversions come in!
Conversion means changing a measurement from one unit to another.
🧠 Why Are Conversions Important?
Imagine you're working with scientists around the world. Some use centimeters, some use meters, and some use kilometers. Converting units helps everyone understand the same thing in a common way.
📏 Common Units and How to Convert Them
1. Length
👉 Example:
Convert 250 cm to meters.
100 cm = 1 m, so:
250 ÷ 100 = 2.5 meters
2. Mass
👉 Example:
Convert 5,000 g to kilograms.
5,000 ÷ 1,000 = 5 kg
3. Time
👉 Example:
Convert 2 hours to minutes.
2 × 60 = 120 minutes
4. Temperature
Physics usually uses Celsius (°C) and sometimes Kelvin (K).
Conversion formula:
K = °C + 273
👉 Example:
If it’s 25°C, then in Kelvin:
25 + 273 = 298 K
🔄 Conversion Tips!
To go from smaller units to larger, divide.
To go from larger units to smaller, multiply.
Always write the unit after the number.

We compute step-by-step.

Average speed = total distance / total time (summary over whole trip).
Instantaneous speed = speed at a single moment (slope of the d–t curve at that time; mathematically the derivative).
Average tells you the overall pace including stops/slow sections. Instantaneous tells you how fast she was going right then.

Equations of Motion – Grade 7 Physics
Equations of motion are formulas that help us describe how things move when they are speeding up, slowing down, or moving at a constant speed. These are especially useful when the acceleration is constant.
Key Terms You Need to Know
The Three Main Equations
1. First Equation:
v = u + at
➤ Final velocity = Initial velocity + (acceleration × time)
2. Second Equation:
s = ut + ½at²
➤ Displacement = (Initial velocity × time) + ½ × acceleration × time²
3. Third Equation:
v² = u² + 2as
➤ Final velocity squared = Initial velocity squared + 2 × acceleration × displacement
Real-Life Example
A bicycle starts from rest (u = 0) and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 4 seconds. What is its final speed and how far does it travel?
Using:
- v = u + at = 0 + 2 × 4 = 8 m/s
- s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 2 × 4² = 16 m
✅ Final speed = 8 m/s
✅ Distance = 16 m

Rules of Significant Figures
Understand the basics of precision in measurements
Rule 1: Non-Zero Digits
All non-zero digits are significant.
Example: 123 → 3 sig figs
45.67 → 4 sig figs
Rule 2: Zeros Between Digits
Zeros between non-zero digits are significant.
Example: 102 → 3 sig figs
1002 → 4 sig figs
Rule 3: Leading Zeros
Leading zeros are not significant.
Example: 0.007 → 1 sig fig
0.00405 → 3 sig figs
Rule 4: Trailing Zeros (Decimal)
Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.
Example: 45.00 → 4 sig figs
3.1400 → 5 sig figs
Rule 5: Trailing Zeros (Whole Number)
Trailing zeros in whole numbers without a decimal are not significant unless specified.
Example: 1200 → 2 sig figs
1200. → 4 sig figs
Bonus: Scientific Notation
Scientific notation always shows only the significant figures.
Example: 4.50 × 10² → 3 sig figs
7.00 × 10⁻³ → 3 sig figs

Speed vs. Velocity: Grade 7 Physics Reading Material
Explore the difference between Speed and Velocity in a fun and clear way.
1. What Is Speed?
• Speed answers the question: How fast are you going?
• It’s just a number (no direction).
• Formula (average speed): Speed = Distance / Time
• Units: m/s (meters per second) or km/h (kilometers per hour)
Example:
A car travels 60 km in 2 hours → Speed = 60 km / 2 h = 30 km/h
2. What Is Velocity?
• Velocity tells you how fast and in which direction.
• It’s a vector (has magnitude + direction).
• Formula (average velocity): Velocity = Displacement / Time
where displacement = straight-line from start to finish in a given direction
Example:
You walk 100 m east in 20 s → Velocity = 100 m / 20 s = 5 m/s east
3. Speed vs. Velocity: Quick Differences
4. Why Does It Matter?
• A car can move at a constant speed along a circle, but its velocity changes because the direction changes.
• If the car returns to starting point, its overall displacement = 0, so average velocity = 0 m/s—even though it was moving the whole time!
5. Real-Life Scenarios & Questions
1️⃣ Walk to the library:
- Distance = 200 m
- Time = 100 seconds
- Speed = 200 ÷ 100 = 2 m/s
- Direction = north
- Velocity = 2 m/s north
2️⃣ Walk to the library then return home (total = 400 m), in 200 seconds:
- Average Speed = 400 ÷ 200 = 2 m/s
- Displacement = 0
- Average Velocity = 0 ÷ 200 = 0 m/s
6. Key Takeaways for Grade 7
• Speed = How fast? (distance ÷ time)
• Velocity = How fast + in which direction? (displacement ÷ time)
• Speed is always positive; velocity can be zero or have sign based on direction.
• Even if you run the same path, your speed can stay the same while velocity changes with direction.
7. Mini Classroom Activity
• Ask students to track a short jog:
- Measure distance and time → compute speed
- Note direction (e.g., north, east) → compute velocity
- Ask: What if they jog back to start? What happens to velocity?
8. Summary
Speed is like a speedometer in a car—just a number.
Velocity is like saying “30 km/h east”—number plus direction.

Types of Errors in Measurement
📌 What is an Error?
In Physics, an error is the difference between the measured value (what we record) and the true value (the actual value of the quantity). Errors don’t mean carelessness — they are natural and happen in all measurements.
1. Systematic Errors
- Happen the same way every time.
- Caused by faulty instruments, wrong calibration, or poor measuring technique.
- Example: If a weighing scale is not set to zero, every reading will show extra weight.
👉 Systematic errors always shift results in one direction (too high or too low).
2. Random Errors
- Happen due to unpredictable changes.
- Caused by small changes in environment or human reaction time.
- Example: Different students starting/stopping a stopwatch at slightly different times.
👉 Random errors make results scatter around the true value.
3. Gross Errors (Human Errors)
- Happen due to carelessness or mistakes while measuring.
- Example: Reading a scale wrongly, writing down 56 instead of 65, or misplacing a decimal point.
👉 Gross errors can be avoided with careful work and double-checking.
4. Parallax Error
- A special error when the eye is not in line with the scale.
- Example: Reading the water level in a cylinder from above or below eye level.
👉 Always keep your eye straight with the marking to avoid parallax error.
✅ How to Reduce Errors
1. Check and calibrate instruments before use.
2. Take multiple readings and calculate the average.
3. Record carefully and double-check values.
4. Use correct scientific methods.
🌟 Key Idea
Errors cannot be completely avoided, but they can be reduced. Good scientists always mention possible errors to show how reliable their results are.

What is Tension?
Tension is a force along the length of a medium, especially a force carried by a flexible medium, such as a rope or cable.
Tension can be defined as an action-reaction pair of forces acting at each end of the said elements. While considering a rope, the tension force is felt by every section of the rope in both the directions, apart from the endpoints. The endpoints experience tension on one side and the force from the weight attached. Throughout the string, the tension varies in some circumstances.
Common Misconception
Tension is a pulling force and not a pushing force as ropes can’t push effectively. Attempting to push the rope will cause the rope to go slack losing the tension it possesses. This might sound obvious but while illustrating the forces acting on an object, people often draw the force of tension going in the wrong direction. Hence it is important to remember that tension can only pull on an object but not push against it.
How do we calculate the force of tension?
The tension on an object is equal to the product of the mass of the object and gravitational force added to the product of the mass and acceleration. Mathematically, it is represented as follows:
T = mg + ma
Let us look at the solved example to better understand.

Solution:

Substituting the values in the equation, we get T= (10 kg) (9.8 m/s2) + (10 kg) (0)

Levers – Simple Machines
A lever is a simple tool that helps us lift or move things easily. It has a strong bar that turns around a fixed point called the fulcrum.
👉 Examples: seesaw, crowbar, bottle opener.
Parts of a Lever
🟩 Remember: Effort helps to move the load. Fulcrum helps the lever turn.
Types of Levers
🟨 Think: Which lever helps you lift heavy things easily? Which one helps you move things faster?
How Levers Help Us
Levers make work easier. They help us use less effort to lift heavy loads. When the effort arm (the side where we push) is longer than the load arm, we get more advantage.
Mechanical Advantage (MA)
Meaning: How much a lever multiplies our effort.
MA = Load ÷ Effort
If a lever lifts 20 N using 5 N effort:
MA = 20 ÷ 5 = 4 → The lever makes our effort 4 times stronger!
Velocity Ratio (VR)
Meaning: How much farther the effort moves compared to the load.
VR = Distance moved by Effort ÷ Distance moved by Load
If you move your hand a long way to lift a heavy box a small distance — that’s VR in action!
Efficiency (η)
Meaning: How well a machine uses your effort.
η = (MA ÷ VR) × 100
No machine is perfect — some energy is lost due to friction.
Example: If MA = 4 and VR = 5, η = (4 ÷ 5) × 100 = 80%. The lever works at 80% efficiency.

- There are three classes of levers.
- MA shows how much a lever multiplies force.

SIMPLE MACHINES
What Is a Simple Machine?
A mechanical device that changes the direction or magnitude of a force is known as a simple machine. In general terms, they are defined as simple mechanisms that use leverage or mechanical advantage to multiply force. Simple machines have few or no moving parts to modify motion and force. Let us learn more about the six simple machines in the following few sections.
The Inclined Plane
An inclined plane is a sloping plane used to raise heavy bodies. Inclined planes make it easier to lift objects to greater heights. There are two ways to raise an object: it can be either raised by lifting it straight up or by pushing it diagonally up.
Lifting an object straight up moves the object in the shortest distance, but a more significant force must be exerted. Using an inclined plane to lift objects requires a smaller force but must be exerted over a long distance. A few everyday examples of inclined planes include sidewalk ramps, highway access ramps, inclined conveyor belts, and switchback roads.
Wheel and Axle
Wheel and axle make work easier by moving objects across distances. The wheel and axle greatly reduce the friction involved in moving an object. The wheel (round ends) turns with the axle (or cylindrical post), causing movement. The wheel is one of the most significant inventions in the history of the world. Before the invention of wheels, the amount of load and the distance through which we could carry the load over land was limited. It is easier to roll a 2000-kilogram block of stone using logs placed underneath it than to push it.
Pulley
Instead of an axle, a wheel could also rotate a rope, cord, or belt. The variation of the wheel and axle is the pulley. In a pulley, a cord wraps around a wheel. It is a simple machine that is used to change the direction of the force. When the wheel rotates, the cord can move in either direction. By attaching a hook to the cord, the wheel’s rotation can be used to raise or lower objects, making work easier. Various types of pulley help in making a variety of lifting and moving tasks easier. A pulley system is necessary to hoist a flag on a flag pole. A rope is attached to the pulley to raise and lower the flag more easily.
Lever
A lever is a tool that is used to pry objects loose. Levers are also used to lift objects. A lever has an arm that turns against a fulcrum. Exerting a force on one end of the pivot creates a force on the other end of the lever. The applied force is either increased or decreased based on the distance from the fulcrum to the load and from the fulcrum to the effort. A few examples of levers are the hammer that we used to pry nails loose and the kids’ favourite see-saw.
Screw
An inclined plane wrapped around a shaft is known as a screw. The two primary functions of a screw are to hold things together or to lift objects. The threading around the shaft in a screw makes it an efficient tool to hold things together. The threads grip the surrounding material like teeth, resulting in a secure hold. A few examples of screws include screw, bolt, clamp, spinning stool, and spiral staircase. A car jack is an example of a screw used to lift something.
Wedge
A wedge is a simple machine that is used to break apart substances by applying force. A nail is a common example of a wedge. In a nail, the force is applied to the wide head area. This applied force is concentrated in a small point area where the force is exerted. At this point, the force is magnified and enables the force to pierce through objects. As the nail sinks into the object, the wedge shape at the point of the nail moves forward and forces the object apart.

SDL:  What is Force?
Push or pull of an object is considered a force. Push and pull come from the objects interacting with one another. Terms like stretch and squeeze can also be used to denote force.
In Physics, force is defined as: The push or pull on an object with mass causes it to change its velocity.
Force is an external agent capable of changing a body’s state of rest or motion. It has a magnitude and a direction. The direction towards which the force is applied is known as the direction of the force, and the application of force is the point where force is applied.
The Force can be measured using a spring balance. The SI unit of force is Newton(N).
What are the Effects of Force?
In physics, motion is defined as the change in position with respect to time. In simpler words, motion refers to the movement of a body. Typically, motion can either be described as:
Change in speed     2. Change in direction
The Force has different effects, and here are some of them.
Force can make a body that is at rest to move.   It can stop a moving body or slow it down.
It can accelerate the speed of a moving body.   It can also change the direction of a moving body along with its shape and size.
