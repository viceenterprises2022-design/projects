Criterion D
Task 1: The Isotope Debate in Your Community
Key Concept: Systems
Related Concepts: Risks, Solutions, Evidence
Global Context: Scientific and technical innovation
Scenario
A local community is considering two new proposals involving the use of radioactive isotopes:
Proposal A (Medical): A new hospital wing that uses Iodine-131 for thyroid treatments and Technetium-99m for medical imaging.
Proposal B (Industry/Food): A food processing plant that uses Cobalt-60 for food irradiation to extend the shelf life of produce and kill harmful bacteria.
The community council is divided and concerned about potential radiation risks and long-term safety. They have asked you, as a student science consultant, to provide a report analyzing the situation.
Questions
Outline how the isotopes Iodine-131 and Cobalt-60 are used in the proposed facilities to address the specific problems of thyroid conditions and food spoilage.
Discuss the effectiveness of using these specific isotopes to solve their respective problems. Consider the benefits, such as the short half-life of Iodine-131 for diagnosis or the effective bacteria-killing properties of Cobalt-60.
Evaluate the overall positive and negative impacts of both proposals on society and the environment, considering factors like health improvements, waste disposal challenges, and potential accident risks.
Develop a strategy the community or the facilities could implement to minimize the negative impacts or risks associated with the use and storage of these isotopes.
Task 2: The Nuclear Energy Production Article Analysis
Key Concept: Change
Related Concepts: Consequences, Energy, Models
Global Context: Fairness and development
Source Article: Nuclear Energy: A Double-Edged Sword for Our Future
By The Science Review (2025)
The world needs massive amounts of energy to power homes, transport, and industry. For decades, burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) has been the primary solution. However, this releases huge amounts of greenhouse gases, contributing significantly to climate change.
Nuclear energy offers a powerful alternative. It is generated in power plants using nuclear fission, typically with an isotope of uranium (U-235) as fuel. Nuclear power plants can generate a large amount of electricity 24/7 (baseload power) and produce near-zero carbon emissions during operation, unlike coal plants. The energy density of uranium is also vastly superior to fossil fuels. Proponents argue nuclear is a very safe energy source when considering deaths per TWh compared to the millions who die annually from air pollution caused by burning fossil fuels.
Despite the benefits, significant challenges exist. Nuclear power plants produce radioactive waste that remains hazardous for thousands of years, requiring highly secure, long-term storage solutions. There is also the risk, though rare, of catastrophic accidents like Chernobyl or Fukushima, which can release dangerous radiation into the environment and displace thousands of people. Furthermore, the plants use vast amounts of water for cooling, discharging hot water back into lakes or oceans, causing "thermal pollution" that harms aquatic ecosystems.
The debate continues: Is it worth the risks to fight climate change?
Questions
Explain how nuclear energy production, as described in the article, is used as a scientific solution to the problem of providing large-scale, consistent electricity with low carbon emissions.
Discuss the effectiveness of nuclear energy as a solution to the energy crisis compared to fossil fuels, referring to the data or benefits mentioned in the article (e.g., reliability, energy density, emissions avoided).
Evaluate the overall positive and negative impacts of relying on nuclear power, making justified judgments using evidence from the article regarding society, the environment, and the economy.
Propose an action or a policy that local governments or nuclear power companies could take to manage the long-term storage of radioactive waste and minimize thermal pollution risks.
