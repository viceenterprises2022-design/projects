1. Writing Formulae of Binary Ionic Compounds
(Using the Criss-Cross Method)
Binary ionic compounds are made of:
one metal ion (positive)
one non-metal ion (negative)
Steps (Criss-Cross Method):
Write the symbols of the ions.
Write their charges.
Criss-cross the charges to become subscripts.
Remove charges and simplify if needed.
Example 1: Magnesium chloride
Magnesium ion: Mg²⁺
Chloride ion: Cl⁻
Criss-cross → Mg₁Cl₂
Correct formula: MgCl₂
Example 2: Aluminium oxide
Aluminium ion: Al³⁺
Oxide ion: O²⁻
Criss-cross → Al₂O₃
Correct formula: Al₂O₃
2. Writing Formulae Using Monatomic Ions
Monatomic ions are ions made from one atom only.
Common monatomic ions:
Na⁺ (sodium)
Ca²⁺ (calcium)
O²⁻ (oxide)
Cl⁻ (chloride)
Example: Calcium oxide
Ca²⁺ and O²⁻
Criss-cross gives Ca₂O₂ → simplify
Correct formula: CaO
Explanation:
Both ions have the same charge, so they combine in a 1:1 ratio.
3. Writing Formulae with Polyatomic Ions
Polyatomic ions are groups of atoms with an overall charge.
They act as one unit when writing formulae.
Common polyatomic ions:
NH₄⁺ → Ammonium
SO₄²⁻ → Sulfate
CO₃²⁻ → Carbonate
NO₃⁻ → Nitrate
OH⁻ → Hydroxide
Important rule:
Use brackets when more than one polyatomic ion is needed.
Example 1: Ammonium sulfate
NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻
Criss-cross → (NH₄)₂SO₄
Correct formula: (NH₄)₂SO₄
Example 2: Calcium carbonate
Ca²⁺ and CO₃²⁻
Charges cancel directly
Correct formula: CaCO₃
4. Writing Formulae of Simple Covalent Compounds
Covalent compounds are formed between non-metals only. Atoms share electrons instead of transferring them.
Rule:
The name tells you the number of atoms using prefixes. In covalent compounds, subscripts come from prefixes, not charges.
Example 1: Carbon dioxide
Carbon (C)
Di-oxide → 2 oxygen atoms
Correct formula: CO₂
Example 2: Dinitrogen monoxide
Di-nitrogen → 2 nitrogen atoms
Mono-oxide → 1 oxygen atom
Correct formula: N₂O
