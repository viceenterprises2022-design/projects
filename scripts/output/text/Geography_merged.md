Location and Climate
Temperate grasslands are large open areas covered mainly by grasses, with very few
trees. They are found in di:erent parts of the world:
•  Prairies – North America
•  Pampas – South America
•  Steppes – Europe and Central Asia
•  Veld – South Africa
The climate of temperate grasslands is usually moderate, with hot summers and cold
winters. Rainfall is low to moderate (25–75 cm per year), which is not enough to support
large forests but good for grass growth. The strong winds and seasonal droughts make
grasses the most suitable vegetation.
2. Adaptations of Plants, Animals, and Indigenous Communities
•  Plants: Grasses grow tall in wetter areas and remain short in drier areas. They
have deep roots to absorb water and survive ﬁres or droughts.
•  Animals: Large herds of grazing animals like bison (in North America) or
antelopes (in Africa) feed on grasses. Predators like wolves and lions survive by
hunting them. Animals often migrate or burrow to escape harsh weather.
•
Indigenous Communities: People living in temperate grasslands have adapted
by practicing pastoralism (herding animals) and farming (wheat, maize, barley).
Their lifestyle often changes with the seasons, depending on water and grazing
availability.
3. Economic Importance, Threats, and Conservation
•  Economic Importance: Grasslands are known as the "breadbaskets of the
world" because they produce large amounts of wheat and maize. They also
support cattle and sheep farming, which provide meat, milk, wool, and leather.
•  Threats: Overgrazing, intensive farming, use of pesticides, and land being
converted into towns or industries are major threats. Climate change also
increases droughts and reduces soil fertility.
•  Conservation Measures:
o  Protecting grasslands as national parks and reserves
o  Practicing sustainable farming and grazing
o  Replanting native grasses and restoring ecosystems
o  Raising awareness about the importance of grasslands

Global Distribution and Climate Patterns
Tropical grasslands, also known as savannas, are found mainly on either side of the
Equator, between tropical rainforests and deserts. Major savanna regions include East
and Central Africa (Kenya, Tanzania), South America (Brazil, Venezuela), Australia,
and parts of India.
The climate of savannas is marked by two distinct seasons:
•  A wet season (about 4–6 months) with heavy rainfall that allows plants to grow.
•  A dry season (about 6–8 months) when little or no rain falls, often leading to
droughts and bushﬁres.
Temperatures are high all year round, but rainfall is seasonal and unevenly distributed.
2. Adaptations of Plants and Animals
Life in the savanna requires special adaptations to survive the extreme wet–dry cycle.
Plants:
•  Trees like acacia and baobab have thick bark and deep roots to store water.
•  Grasses grow quickly in the wet season but become brown and dormant during
the dry season to conserve water.
•  Many plants have ﬁre-resistant features to survive frequent bushﬁres.
Animals:
•  Large herbivores like elephants, zebras, and giraHes migrate to ﬁnd water and
fresh grass.
•  Carnivores like lions and cheetahs adapt their hunting to follow herds.
•  Smaller animals (e.g., termites, burrowing rodents) live underground to escape
heat and predators.
3. Ecological Importance, Threats, and Conservation
Savannas are among the most biodiverse ecosystems on Earth, supporting thousands
of plant and animal species. They are also important for:
•  Carbon storage through grasslands and trees.
•  Tourism (e.g., African safaris).
•
Indigenous communities, who rely on grasslands for grazing, food, and shelter.
However, savannas face serious threats:
•  Deforestation for farming and cattle ranching.
•  Overgrazing, which damages soil and vegetation.
•  Climate change, leading to longer dry seasons and more frequent droughts.
•  Poaching and loss of wildlife habitats.
Conservation strategies include:
•  Establishing protected areas and national parks.
•  Promoting sustainable grazing and farming practices.
•  Reforestation and ﬁre management programs.
•  Educating communities and supporting eco-tourism.

What Are The Resources Of the Amazon Rainforest?
The largest tropical rainforest on Earth, the Amazon provides one of the richest
examples of biodiversity on the planet. Rainforest resources provide numerous beneﬁts
that impact the entire planet, from producing copious amounts of oxygen to storing
carbon and reducing the impacts of global climate change.
However, human activity – particularly in association with the acquisition of non-
renewable resources – has historically threatened the long-term survival of this crucial
rainforest.
Non-Renewable Resources in the Amazon Rainforest
The Amazon produces a number of rainforest resources, including precious minerals
like gold and copper, as well as cattle and hydroelectric energy. While these Amazon
resources bring huge economic beneﬁts to the region, they also damage the rainforest
in the process. The activity surrounding the production of these resources directly
accounts for huge amounts of deforestation, pollution and displacement of wildlife.
Mining in the Amazon Rainforest
The Amazon produces several diFerent valuable minerals. Researchers estimate that
South America produces 20 percent of Earth's iron ore, which is a valuable component
in construction and machinery. South America also contains about 25 percent of
Earth's copper stores, which people use in the production of electrical wiring.
Mining activities in the Amazon also produce tin and gold. Gold mining in particular
causes extensive damage to the forest. Miners illegally burn sections of forest to rapidly
access the ground, and use toxic mercury during the gold separation process, polluting
waterways.
Deforestation in the Amazon: Cattle Ranching
Cattle ranching accounts for the majority of deforestation in the Amazon. Ranchers
clear sections of forest to create more appropriate grazing land for cattle. The rate of
deforestation in the Amazon actually increased in 2020, with 4,281 square miles of
rainforest cleared. Mining also makes up a large portion of deforestation, as does the
timber industry.
Hydroelectric Energy From the Amazon River
While hydroelectric energy is actually a renewable resource, the construction of dams
used in the production of hydroelectric energy adversely impacts the surrounding forest
and the wildlife within. Damming a river results in the displacement of the people living
in the surrounding area as well. The dam ﬂoods the surrounding rainforest and prevents
aquatic species from travelling into diFerent regions of the river.
Renewable Resources in the Amazon Rainforest
The renewable resources in the Amazon impact not just the local economy, but the
entire world. The Amazon rainforest plays an important part in the climate of our planet
and produces a large percentage of our global oxygen supply. It also holds a large
portion of our fresh water, and contains an immense variety of plants and animals.
Water, Oxygen and Carbon in the Amazon Rainforest
The Amazon River and its surrounding tributaries contain 20 percent of Earth's fresh
water. This watery habitat also supports numerous ﬁsh and wildlife species that the
local people rely on for food.
In addition to fresh water, the Amazon also produces 20 percent of our oxygen. The
millions of trees in the forest take in carbon dioxide emissions and release oxygen into
the atmosphere. As deforestation removes the forest, global carbon emissions
increase, worsening the impacts of climate change.
Rainforest Resources: Medicine
Scientists have identiﬁed approximately 40,000 diFerent species of plants in the
Amazon rainforest. Of those species, researchers have only evaluated a tiny percentage
for their potential medicinal value. Several important medicines derive from Amazonian
plants, including quinine, tubocurarine, vincristine and vinblastine. Scientists use these
medicines as anaesthesia and to treat cancer and malaria.
Ecotourism in the Amazon
Ecotourism provides locals with an alternative to deforestation and destruction of their
rainforest. By conserving the ecosystems around them, ecotourism expansion can
provide people in these regions with a new revenue stream and source of income.
Accessibility remains one of the biggest hurdles for ecotourism in the Amazon.

Conservation of Resources:
Conservation is like taking care of our toys or belongings so that they last longer and we can continue to enjoy them. When we conserve resources, we make sure to use them wisely and not waste them, just like we take care of our favourite things.
Analogies:
Imagine you have a jar of your favourite candies. If you eat them all in one day, you won't have any left for the next day. But if you eat just a few each day and save some for later, your candies will last longer, and you can enjoy them over time. This is similar to conserving resources - using them in a way that they don't run out too quickly.
Think of a water balloon game. If everyone keeps filling and throwing water balloons without any care, the water will run out very quickly, and the game will end. But if everyone fills their water balloons only when needed and tries not to waste them, the game can continue for a long time. This shows how conserving resources can help us have fun for longer.
Relevance and Application:
Conservation of resources is highly relevant and has wide-ranging applications that are important for our environment, economy, and sustainability. Here are some key points:
Environmental Protection: Conservation helps protect our environment by preserving natural resources such as forests, water, and wildlife habitats. It ensures that ecosystems remain balanced and healthy, which is crucial for the well-being of plants, animals, and humans.
Sustainable Energy: Conserving energy resources like electricity and fuel helps reduce pollution and the use of non-renewable resources. Turning off lights when not needed, using energy-efficient appliances, and carpooling are examples of conserving energy and promoting sustainability.
Waste Reduction: By conserving resources, we can minimize waste generation. Recycling and reusing items instead of throwing them away can help reduce the amount of waste that ends up in landfills, protecting the environment and conserving raw materials.
Economic Efficiency: Conservation can lead to economic efficiency by reducing costs and improving resource management. For example, businesses that conserve water or energy can save money on their utility bills. Additionally, efficient use of resources can lead to more sustainable and resilient economies.

UNDERSTANDING THE IMPORTANCE OF SOIL AS A NATURAL RESOURCE
Soil is a valuable and essential natural resource that supports various life forms and plays a critical
role in sustaining ecosystems and human societies. It is a complex mixture of minerals, organic
matter, water, air, and living organisms. Here is an overview of the importance of soil as a natural
resource:
1.  Food Production: Soil is the foundation for agriculture and food production. It provides the
necessary nutrients and a stable medium for plant growth. Nutrient-rich soil supports the
cultivation of crops, fruits, vegetables, and other plants that form the basis of our food
supply.
2.  Nutrient Cycling: Soil acts as a reservoir and regulator of essential nutrients for plants and
organisms. It stores and releases nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium,
facilitating their cycling through the soil-plant system. This nutrient cycling is vital for
sustaining plant growth and maintaining soil fertility.
3.  Water Filtration and Storage: Soil plays a crucial role in water management. It acts as a filter,
removing impurities and pollutants as water percolates through the soil layers. Moreover,
soil acts as a sponge, absorbing and storing water, which helps regulate water availability for
plants, groundwater recharge, and maintaining streamflow.
4.  Biodiversity Support: Soil is a habitat for a diverse range of organisms, including bacteria,
fungi, insects, worms, and other microorganisms. This rich biodiversity in the soil contributes
to nutrient cycling, decomposition of organic matter, pest control, and the overall health and
resilience of ecosystems.
5.  Carbon Sequestration: Soil serves as a significant carbon sink, absorbing and storing carbon
from the atmosphere. Through processes like photosynthesis and decomposition of organic
matter, carbon is incorporated into the soil, mitigating the effects of climate change by
reducing greenhouse gas concentrations.
6.  Erosion Prevention: Healthy soil with sufficient organic matter helps prevent soil erosion. The
root systems of plants hold the soil together, preventing it from being washed away by wind
or water. Soil erosion control is essential for maintaining agricultural productivity, preventing
sedimentation in water bodies, and protecting infrastructure.
7.  Construction and Infrastructure: Soil provides the foundation for construction and
infrastructure development. Its engineering properties, such as strength, stability, and
permeability, determine the feasibility and durability of structures like buildings, roads, and
bridges.
8.  Cultural and Historical Importance: Soil is deeply intertwined with human cultures and
history. It serves as a source of artistic materials, including clay for pottery and pigments for
art. Soil types and properties have also influenced settlement patterns, agriculture practices,
and the development of civilizations throughout history.
9.  Recreation and Aesthetics: Soil contributes to recreational activities and aesthetics. It
provides the medium for gardening, landscaping, and outdoor activities. The texture, color,
and composition of soil can enhance the visual appeal of parks, gardens, and natural
landscapes.
10. Pharmaceutical and Biotechnological Applications: Soil is a source of diverse microorganisms
and natural compounds with potential pharmaceutical and biotechnological applications.
Soil-based research has led to the discovery of antibiotics, enzymes, and other bioactive
substances used in medicine and industry.
Understanding the importance of soil as a natural resource is essential for sustainable land
management, conservation, and promoting practices that preserve soil health and fertility.
Protecting and managing soil resources is crucial for ensuring food security, ecosystem sustainability,
and the well-being of present and future generations.

Introduction to soil health
Soil health is the capacity of soil to function as a vital living ecosystem that sustains its
functions. Soil is a complex environment with many components, including organic
matter, communities of micro-organisms (microbes), air and water pores, and
minerals.
Sadly, human activities can damage soil health impacting entire ecosystems. However,
some agricultural practices, such as regenerative farming, or integrated pest
management, can help protect and restore soil, so it can maintain its vital functions.
In this article we explore soil health, why it is important, how to improve it and future
strategies.
The functions of soil health
Healthy soil sustains essential functions such as:
•  Water regulation: stores water and regulates movement.
•  Support for plant and animal life: contributes to biodiversity and productivity of
living organisms.
•  Pollutant ﬁltering: minerals and soil microbes ﬁltrate pollutants, which protect
groundwater quality.
•  Nutrient cycling: soil microbes participate in the storage and cycling of nutrients
like phosphorus, nitrogen and carbon.
•  Physical stability and support: acts as a medium for plants to grow in and
support human construction.
Understanding the physical, chemical, and biological aspects of a healthy soil
Soil health is a combination of three main soil properties:
•  Physical properties: visual indicators, which include soil structure, aggregate
stability, crusting and compaction, permeability, texture, porosity, etc.
•  Chemical properties: they represent the chemical processes happening in the
soil, for instance, nutrient availability, pH, salinity, etc.
•  Biological properties: include visual indicators such as macro-organisms like
earthworms, collembola, mites, etc. and their activity and byproducts.
All these aspects interact dynamically and can foster healthy soil together. For instance,
soil structure inﬂuences water retention, which creates a good environment for
microorganisms.
A farmer holding soil ﬁlled with earthworms, which are vital for good soil health. Credit:
iStock
Why is soil health important?
Soil is the foundation of agriculture and sustains life on earth by providing nutrients,
clean air and water. Healthy soil helps produce robust plants, increasing crop yields,
food production and food security. Heathy soil also stores carbon from the atmosphere,
mitigating climate change.
Unfortunately, soil health is under threat worldwide due to several drivers, including
intensive agriculture, deforestation, erosion, pollution, and improper fertilizer and
application. A recent study estimates that 61% of lands in the European Union are
aPected by soil degradation, while globally, the FAO reports that 33% of soils are
already degraded. In addition, the IPBES states that over 90% of soils could become
degraded by 2050.
The consequences of soil degradation and poor soil health go well beyond the loss of
good soil for farming. They aPect various aspects of human well-being and the
environment. For instance, as degraded soil holds less water, it can decrease the
amount of water available for plants and worsen drought.
What is soil degradation?
Soil degradation is the physical, chemical and biological decline in soil quality, which
can be caused by human activities or natural processes. Factors inﬂuencing soil
degradation include:
•  Soil compaction by heavy farm machinery, excessive tillage and leaving the soil
bare. Compacted soil does not have enough space for the movement of air,
water and plant roots.
•  Soil erosion or the loss of the top layer of soil at a rate faster than it is formed.
This layer is the fertile portion of soil and is essential for plant growth. Erosion is
mainly due to soil being exposed to climatic conditions after tillage and
deforestation. Soil erosion can also increase pollution in water bodies.
•  Chemical pollution from pesticides, fertilizers and other industrial chemicals.
This negatively impacts soil microbes, decreases the amount and diversity of soil
organisms, and reduces soil fertility.
•  Tillage that releases carbon into the atmosphere, contributing to the emissions
of greenhouse gases and causing poor health of soil. Overall, this contributes to
climate change.
As a result of soil degradation, soil quality gets poorer in nutrients and loses its capacity
to hold water. This causes food production to decrease; in the worst cases, degraded
soil cannot support plant growth anymore.
We must protect soil health at all costs so that soils can sustain its functions,
safeguarding the environment and human well-being.
How can we protect soil health?
Maintaining or creating healthy soil has many beneﬁts. See some of our recommended
techniques, supported by science, to protect this vital life source for both commercial
and private lands.
How to improve soil health for gardening
You can promote soil health at home in your own garden by:
•  Creating compost from food waste, grass clipping or plant debris, for instance.
In doing so, you can avoid the overuse of chemical fertilizers.
•  Mulching increases the soil organic matter and promotes the activity of soil
microbes.
•  Using biological control are nature-based environmentally-friendly
products that protect your garden from pests and diseases and improve plant
health.

•  Reduce the use of chemicals by opting for nature-based solutions

The role of organic matter in soil health
Organic matter is any material produced originally by living organisms (plants and
animals) and is returned to the soil for further decomposition.
Rich soil with organic matter has biodiversity, like bacteria, viruses and fungi, but also
macro-organisms such as beneﬁcial insects or worms.
The presence of organic matter in soil is essential as a source of nutrients for macro-
and microbial organisms. These organisms break down nutrients that are absorbed by
plants.
The importance of organic matter and biodiversity in soil
Organic matter helps soil to function and inﬂuences it, directly and indirectly, in many
ways, such as:
•
Improving soil structure
•  Regulating soil pH, temperature and aeration
•  Promoting microbial activity and diversity
•  Supplies nutrients essential for plant growth
•  Enhancing disease suppression
•  Enhancing water absorption and retention
•  Reducing soil erosion, avoiding the loss of fertile lands.
Organic matter is therefore vital for enhancing overall soil health and fertility and
increasing soil biodiversity. Likely, soil biodiversity is essential for the cycling of
nutrients and water as well as pest suppression, all of which support plant growth and
health. In other words, soil biodiversity provides us with food and clean water.
The dynamics between organic matter and nutrient cycling
Diagram illustrating the nutrient cycle: organic matter is decomposed by
microorganisms, producing nutrients that can be assimilated by plants. © CABI
Organic matter is a reservoir of nutrients that are slowly released into the soil through its
decomposition over time. Microbial organisms decompose organic matter to break
down these essential nutrients into a form that plants can absorb. As a result, soil
fertility increases, and plants can grow better and stronger. Proper management of
organic matter ensures a balanced nutrient cycle. This will contribute to long-term crop
productivity and a balanced ecosystem.
From composting to mulching: Practical approaches to increase soil organic
matter
Several practical approaches increase soil organic matter in gardening and agricultural
systems. Two ways to do this are via:
•  Composting: once fully decomposed, compost becomes organic matter full of
nutrients that can return to the soil.
•  Mulching: by applying any material to the soil to cover it. Wood chips, shavings,
bark or plant residues can be used. As the mulch covers the soil surface, the soil
retains moisture more ePectively, weeds have more diPiculty germinating and
growing, and soil microbial ﬂora increases.
The impact of soil structure on water regulation and plant growth
Soil structure is a fundamental aspect of soil health, directly inﬂuencing its ability to
regulate water and support plant growth.
Soil structure refers to the aggregates of soil particles and pores (or spaces) between
them. A well-structured, healthy soil has large pore spaces that allow water to penetrate
the soil rather than run oP it, air to circulate better, and plant root to reach nutrients and
water more ePectively.
On the contrary, compacted or poorly structured soil restricts water movement and root
growth, leading to reduced plant growth. Over time, these factors contribute to soil
degradation, further diminishing soil health.
Soil health and climate change
Soil health and climate change are intricately linked. Climate change aPects soils
through extreme weather events, higher temperatures, or changes in precipitation
patterns, to name a few. These impact soil health in many ways. For instance:
•  Reduced amount of organic matter from a high rate of decomposition
•  Disturbed soil structure
•  Reduced soil moisture due to high temperatures
•
Increased erosion
Soil health also profoundly impacts climate. Healthy soil can absorb carbon from the
atmosphere (CO2) through carbon sequestration and store it in the soil, eventually
limiting greenhouse gas emissions. However, unhealthy soil contributes to the emission
of CO2 or methane into the atmosphere in several ways, including the loss of organic
matter. Unhealthy soil also sequesters less carbon, aggravating the impact of climate
change.
Therefore, good soil health is one vital part of helping tackle climate change.
The future of soil health: innovations and strategies
Given the importance of maintaining soil health, advances in technology give an
opportunity to utilize these new mediums to help monitor, record and protect soil.
Technological advances in soil health assessment and management
Additionally, precision agriculture and smart technologies are starting to transform how
farming systems are managed. In terms of soil health, tools such as soil sensors,
drones, and satellite imagery can collect lots of soil variables that can support farmers
in making informed decisions.
Measurable soil variables include, for instance, moisture levels, soil pH, and nutrient
content. Information from these measures can help adjust irrigation schedules,
optimize fertilizer application, and more. These technologies are not only improving
crop yields but also promoting soil conservation and long-term health.

A promising development is the recent introduction of US policy to protect soil health,
promoting sustainable agricultural practices to improve food security and combat

and state levels.
For instance, the USDA’s Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) has several
programmes that provide technical and ﬁnancial assistance to farmers and landowners

The 2018 Farm Bill, extended in 2023, is one of the country’s most signiﬁcant funding in
the conservation and management of farmlands. It provides programmes that support
healthy soil practices.
Global movement towards soil health and sustainability
Soil health is increasingly recognized as a cornerstone of sustainability. There is a
growing movement towards prioritizing soil health through practices such as
regenerative agriculture, agroecology and climate-smart agriculture. Global initiatives,
such as the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the Global Soil
Partnership, emphasize the critical role of healthy soil in achieving food security and
tackling climate change.
Countries worldwide are adopting policies and practices that promote soil
conservation. Collaborative ePorts among governments, NGOs, and the private sector
are driving research, education, and the implementation of sustainable soil
management techniques, fostering a global commitment to preserving and enhancing
soil health.
By embracing these sustainable practices and fostering global cooperation, we can

What is soil?
Soil is the thin layer of material covering the earth’s surface and is formed from the
weathering of rocks. It is made up mainly of mineral particles, organic materials, air,
water and living organisms—all of which interact slowly yet constantly.
Most plants get their nutrients from the soil and they are the main source of food for
humans, animals and birds. Therefore, most living things on land depend on soil for
their existence.
Soil is a valuable resource that needs to be carefully managed as it is easily damaged,
washed or blown away. If we understand soil and manage it properly, we will avoid
destroying one of the essential building blocks of our environment and our food
security.

other chemicals.
•  biological weathering—the breakdown of rocks by living things. Burrowing
animals help water and air get into rock, and plant roots can grow into cracks in
the rock, making it split.

The Brandt Line gave us the Global North and South. It needs an update.
In 1980, Willy Brandt drew a line across the map that still inﬂuences how we think
about the world.
In 1980, a German politician divided the world in a Global North and a Global South. In
2024, the world is still unequal, but the line no longer applies.
Key Takeaways
Historically, things don’t end well when German Chancellors start drawing lines on
maps. Could the Brandt Line be the exception that proves the rule?
To be fair, Willy Brandt was no longer Chancellor of West Germany (as he had been from
1969 to 1974) when he drew this line. In 1980, a commission chaired by Brandt
published a report on international development that introduced the Global North and
the Global South as meaningful concepts in public discourse.
The line that divides both — indicating a large gap in GDP per capita between the two
parts of the world — became known as the Brandt Line. As this map shows, it divides
the world into two parts, but it doesn’t neatly separate the north from the south.
In the Americas, the Brandt Line follows the jagged U.S.-Mexican border and leaves all
of Latin America and the Caribbean in the Global South.
Europe is entirely to the north of the line, except for Cyprus and Turkey. All the (now
former) Soviet republics are in the Global North, as is Japan. But China, along with
North and South Korea, is in the Global South, as is the rest of Asia, and the entirety of
Africa.
Despite bends and curves, the Brandt Line clearly separates a richer north from a
poorer south, with one glaring exception — or rather, two: Australia and New Zealand,
both advanced economies in the Southern Hemisphere, hence the hedging terminology
that allows those countries to be in the Global North while being in the actual south.
The Brandt Report did more than highlight the world’s North-South divide — it also
proposed solutions to the worldwide economic imbalance: The Global North should
give economic aid to the Global South, so it could catch up and narrow the wealth gap.
Obviously, that hasn’t happened, and you could argue that is either because the aid has
been insuaicient or because aid is not the right instrument to help economies mature.
Whatever the right answer, things have changed since 1980. While the global wealth gap
persists, the Brandt Line no longer reﬂects the reality of the divide.
The UN’s 2022 Human Development Index: green is “very high” (>0.8), yellow is “high”
(0.7-0.799), orange is “medium” (0.55-0.699), and red is “low” (<0.549).
In the four decades since the former Chancellor’s report, an almost continuous
economic boom has transformed the economies of both China and South Korea, lifting
most of their populations out of poverty.
The transformation has been equally impressive in parts of the Middle East. Just put a
picture of Dubai in 1980 next to one from today. The rapid economic development of
Mexico, Brazil, India, and other countries in the so-called Global South belies the use of
that term as a euphemism for the “Third World,” incapable of closing the gap with the
First World economies in Europe and North America.
Perhaps the Brandt Line was always too blunt an instrument to understand global
inequality. These days, it certainly is outdated. But could it be overhauled to reﬂect
today’s reality? Reddit contributor zzz_ch has drawn a revised version of the map, using
data from the UN’s 2023 Human Development Index (HDI) as a yardstick.
Countries with an HDI above 0.8 are in the Global North, and those with a lower HDI are
in the Global South. An HDI of 0.8 is considered “very high.” Bulgaria falls just below,
and Belarus just makes the grade.

Factors Affecting Development
Development refers to the progress of a country in terms of economic growth, social well-being, and environmental sustainability. Various physical and human factors influence the development of a country, shaping its ability to grow and improve living conditions for its people. These factors can be broadly categorized into physical (natural) factors and human (socio-economic and political) factors.
1. Physical Factors Affecting Development
1.1 Climate and Weather Patterns
Areas with extreme climates (very hot, very cold, or highly unpredictable rainfall) tend to have slower development.
Moderate climates with predictable rainfall and temperatures support agriculture and human habitation.
Harsh conditions like droughts, floods, or hurricanes can disrupt economies and lead to food insecurity.
1.2 Natural Resources
Countries rich in natural resources (oil, gas, minerals, fertile soil) have the potential for high economic growth if resources are managed well.
Resource-rich countries like Norway (oil) or Canada (minerals) benefit from exports.
However, poor management can lead to the resource curse (e.g., Nigeria, where oil wealth has not led to widespread development).
1.3 Geographical Location
Landlocked countries often struggle with trade since they depend on neighboring countries for port access. Example: Chad, Bolivia.
Coastal countries benefit from easier trade and tourism. Example: Singapore, UAE.
1.4 Physical Features (Mountains, Rivers, Plains, and Landforms)
Mountains can hinder infrastructure development and trade (e.g., Nepal, Afghanistan).
Rivers and plains provide fertile land for agriculture and ease transportation (e.g., the Nile River in Egypt).
1.5 Vulnerability to Natural Disasters
Countries prone to earthquakes, tsunamis, and hurricanes face setbacks in development due to frequent destruction of infrastructure. Example: Haiti, Bangladesh.
Strong disaster management systems (e.g., Japan’s earthquake preparedness) help mitigate economic losses.
1.6 Diseases and Health Conditions
Tropical regions with high prevalence of diseases (e.g., malaria, dengue, Ebola) see lower productivity and higher healthcare costs.
Better healthcare systems support economic growth and human development (e.g., Cuba, which has a strong healthcare system).
2. Human Factors Affecting Development
2.1 Population Size and Growth Rates
Rapid population growth can strain resources, leading to unemployment and poverty (e.g., India, Nigeria).
Aging populations create challenges for economic productivity and healthcare (e.g., Japan, Italy).
2.2 Education and Literacy Rates
Access to quality education leads to an educated workforce, innovation, and better job opportunities.
Countries with high literacy rates tend to have stronger economies (e.g., Finland, South Korea).
Poor education systems limit economic progress and social mobility (e.g., Afghanistan).
2.3 Healthcare and Life Expectancy
Good healthcare systems improve life expectancy and productivity, leading to sustained development.
Poor healthcare results in high infant mortality and low labor productivity (e.g., Sub-Saharan Africa).
2.4 Political Stability and Governance
Strong, transparent governments attract investment and ensure sustainable policies (e.g., Canada, Switzerland).
Corrupt or unstable governments hinder progress and deter foreign investment (e.g., Venezuela, Somalia).
2.5 Economic Policies and Industrialization
Countries that promote industrialization and diversification of the economy tend to develop faster (e.g., China, South Korea).
Over-reliance on a single industry (e.g., oil in Venezuela) can lead to economic collapse.
2.6 Infrastructure and Technology
Good infrastructure (roads, railways, internet access) boosts trade and economic activities.
Technological advancements lead to higher productivity and innovation (e.g., Japan, USA).
Poor infrastructure slows down economic activities (e.g., rural Africa).
2.7 Social and Cultural Factors
Gender equality, social norms, and cultural beliefs can either promote or hinder development.
Societies that support women’s empowerment and equal opportunities tend to develop faster.
Discriminatory practices limit the workforce and economic progress (e.g., limited rights for women in some countries).
2.8 Globalization and International Trade
Open economies that participate in international trade tend to grow faster (e.g., Singapore).
Protectionist policies and trade restrictions can slow down development.
Conclusion
Both physical and human factors play a critical role in shaping a country’s development. While physical factors like climate, natural resources, and geography create the foundation for economic activities, human factors such as education, healthcare, governance, and industrialization determine how effectively a country can utilize its resources. Successful development depends on a combination of good governance, investment in human capital, and sustainable management of natural resources.

What Is Globalization?
Globalization means that countries depend on each other for trade. This includes
buying and selling goods and services. It also involves sharing technology, investments,
and information with international audiences.
In business, globalization provides access to capital, labour, and resources. An auto
manufacturer might design a car in Japan. They could produce parts in the United
States. Finally, they may have it assembled in Mexico.
Yet cultural, political, and linguistic diEerences can make it diEicult to enter new
markets. To increase the odds of success, companies adapt products to meet the
needs and preferences of local buyers through a process known as localization. Think of
localization as one piece of a wider globalization strategy.
Factors A4ecting Globalization
Globalization is inﬂuenced by various factors, including:
•  Technological Advancements: The development of communication and
transportation technologies has facilitated global integration.
•  Economic Interdependence: Trade liberalization, foreign direct investment, and
the interconnectedness of global ﬁnancial markets have increased economic
interdependence among countries.
•  Political Factors: Government policies, international agreements, and
geopolitical shifts can either promote or hinder globalization.
•  Cultural Exchange: The exchange of ideas, values, and cultural practices
through media, travel, and migration contributes to globalization.
•  Environmental Concerns: Environmental issues such as climate change and

information through social media impact globalization.

multinational corporations shape the global economy.

The Pros and Cons of Globalization
Today, website globalization—and the localization it sparks—is growing faster than ever.
We are entering a new phase of the digital economy. This phase involves advanced
technology and cybercrimes. It is important for us to understand the global impact of
these changes.
Pros of Globalization
1. Access to New Markets
Website globalization gives businesses the opportunity to expand into new markets,
reach international buyers, and increase revenue.
Over time, companies can experience saturation for demand of their products or
services domestically. By expanding globally, they can continue growing by meeting
foreign demand and improving the user experience.
2. Spread of Knowledge and Technology
In order to cooperate globally, companies must share similar technology and a
technological structure. E-commerce, for example, allows companies to sell products
worldwide through Amazon.com.
Similarly, a centralized base of knowledge allows companies to quickly transfer
information and develop innovative solutions. For example, new website globalization
technology helps healthcare products reach multilingual target markets faster. This
includes new medicines and medical devices.
3. Enhanced Global Cooperation and Tolerance
Globalization enhances cooperation by enabling countries to specialize. This allows
them to leverage their economic strengths and trade those products for other
resources. For example, a country in South America that grows sugar cane can sell it to
a developed country. In return, it can receive manufactured goods.
On an interpersonal level, studies have shown that globalization promotes tolerance, as
people are exposed to new cultures and network with others across the globe.
4. Promotes Economic Growth
Studies have found that globalization enhances economic growth by distributing
resources more eEiciently because countries can specialize in activities with
comparative advantages. It also promotes growth indirectly through complementary
reforms in terms of capital and ﬁnancial development.

Cons of Globalization
1. Increased Competition
Although free trade can increase a nation’s wealth, it also increases competition. Local
businesses must compete with multinational corporations that produce cheaper goods
at lower costs, which puts them at a disadvantage.
At the same time, the increase in choices impacts buying behaviours, as customers
expect high quality products at low prices. That means companies must continuously
adapt to meet demands.
2. Exploitation of Labor and Resources
Wealthy, industrialized nations sometimes enter trade agreements with developing
countries in order to exploit weak labour and environmental laws. For example, the

goods.
Lack of environmental regulations in some developing countries also allows developed
countries to import resources such as precious metals at lower prices. This results in
both lasting environmental damage and human rights abuses.
3. Imbalanced Trade
A trade imbalance, also known as a trade deﬁcit, occurs when a country spends more
on imports than it makes on exports. This creates a shortfall in capital that the country
must make up for either by borrowing money from foreign lenders or permitting foreign
investments in its assets.
While lending and investment help promote economic growth, these strategies can be
risky—especially for a developing country. Throughout the 1990s, Thailand, Indonesia,
and Malaysia ran large trade deﬁcits and relied on foreign capital to make up for it. Yet
when the Asian ﬁnancial crisis hit in 1997, foreign investors backed out, leaving these
countries in a precarious ﬁnancial position.
4. Domestic Job Loss
When industrialized countries outsource labour, it causes a shortage of jobs
domestically. Laborers whose skills are no longer in demand experience higher
unemployment, and struggle to adapt to the changing labour market.
For example, the U.S. trade deﬁcit with China eliminated 3.7 million jobs between 2001
and 2018, and more than 75% of those losses were in manufacturing.

Indicators of Development
1. GDP (Gross Domestic Product)
Explanation: GDP measures the total value of all goods and services produced within a country’s borders. It is used to assess the economic strength of a nation.
Example: If India's GDP is $3.7 trillion, it means that the total value of goods and services produced within India in that year amounts to $3.7 trillion.
Types of GDP:
Nominal GDP: Measured using current market prices.
Real GDP: Adjusted for inflation.
GDP per capita: GDP divided by population to assess individual prosperity.
2. GNP (Gross National Product)
Explanation: GNP includes GDP but also adds income earned by a country’s citizens abroad while excluding income earned by foreigners within the country.
Example: If a U.S.-based company has factories in China and Germany, the income from these factories is included in U.S. GNP, but not in U.S. GDP.
Formula:
GNP=GDP+Net Income from Abroad
3. HDI (Human Development Index)
Explanation: HDI is a measure of overall development, not just economic growth. It includes:
Life expectancy (health and longevity)
Education (mean and expected years of schooling)
Income (GNI per capita, adjusted for purchasing power parity - PPP)
Example:
Norway (HDI = 0.961) – High life expectancy, good education, high income.
Nigeria (HDI = 0.539) – Lower life expectancy, education, and income.
Scale: Ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 being the highest human development.
4. Per Capita Income
Explanation: It represents the average income per person in a country and helps compare the standard of living across nations.
Example:
The USA's per capita income is around $70,000, meaning that on average, each citizen earns this much annually.
India’s per capita income is around $2,600, which shows a lower income distribution compared to the USA.
Formula:
Per Capita Income=Total Population/Total National Income​
5.  North-South Brandt Line
Explanation: The Brandt Line was proposed in the 1980s by German Chancellor Willy Brandt to highlight the economic gap between wealthy (Global North) and developing (Global South) nations.
Characteristics:
Global North: High GDP, industrialized, strong economies (USA, Canada, Europe, Japan, Australia).
Global South: Lower GDP, developing economies, reliance on agriculture and raw materials (India, Brazil, Nigeria, Bangladesh).
Example:
Germany (Global North): High industrialization, high HDI.
Kenya (Global South): Developing economy, lower income and HDI.
Relevance Today: Some argue that the Brandt Line is outdated as countries like China and India have become major economic powers. However, economic disparities still exist globally.

Poverty: Causes and Impacts
Source : https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/poverty.asp
1. What is Poverty?
Poverty is the condition where people lack the financial resources and essentials to maintain a minimum standard of living. It leads to hunger, poor health, lack of education, and limited opportunities for economic and social advancement.
There are two main types of poverty:
Absolute Poverty: When people cannot afford basic necessities like food, clean water, shelter, and healthcare. The World Bank defines extreme poverty as living on less than $2.15 per day.
Relative Poverty: When people earn significantly less than the average income in their country, leading to lower living standards and social exclusion.
2. Causes of Poverty
Poverty arises from multiple factors, including economic, social, political, and environmental conditions.
A. Economic Causes
Unemployment & Underemployment – Lack of jobs or low-paying jobs prevents individuals from earning a stable income.
Low Wages & Inequality – In many countries, the gap between the rich and poor continues to widen due to unequal distribution of wealth.
Economic Recessions – Financial crises lead to job losses, inflation, and reduced investment in social welfare.
Lack of Industrial Development – Some economies rely on agriculture or informal sectors, which are unstable and offer low wages.
Inflation & Rising Costs – The rising cost of living makes it difficult for low-income families to afford basic necessities.
B. Social Causes
Lack of Education – Poor education limits job opportunities, creating a cycle of poverty across generations.
Overpopulation – Rapid population growth increases pressure on resources, leading to higher unemployment and food shortages.
Health Problems & Diseases – Poor access to healthcare leads to high medical expenses, reducing disposable income.
Discrimination & Social Exclusion – Marginalized groups (women, ethnic minorities, disabled individuals) often face economic disadvantages.
C. Political Causes
Corruption & Mismanagement – Governments failing to distribute wealth fairly result in poor infrastructure, low wages, and lack of welfare programs.
Wars & Conflicts – Displaced populations lose homes, jobs, and access to education and healthcare.
Weak Governance & Unstable Policies – Ineffective social protection policies fail to address poverty issues.
D. Environmental Causes
Climate Change & Natural Disasters – Droughts, floods, and hurricanes destroy homes and agricultural land, pushing people into poverty.
Deforestation & Land Degradation – The destruction of forests and soil depletion affects rural communities dependent on farming.
Lack of Clean Water & Sanitation – Poor sanitation leads to diseases, affecting productivity and health.
3. Impacts of Poverty
Poverty affects individuals and societies in multiple ways:
A. Social Impacts
Hunger & Malnutrition – Poor nutrition leads to stunted growth, poor brain development, and weakened immunity.
Lower Life Expectancy – Poor healthcare access results in higher infant mortality and shorter lifespans.
Crime & Violence – Poverty drives people toward illegal activities like theft, drug trafficking, and human trafficking.
Education Gap – Many children from poor families drop out of school to work and support their families.
B. Economic Impacts
Low Productivity – Poor health and lack of education reduce the efficiency of workers, slowing down economic growth.
Reduced Consumer Spending – Low-income populations cannot afford goods and services, leading to slower business growth.
Increased Government Debt – Governments spend more on welfare programs and healthcare but may not have enough tax revenue.
C. Political Impacts
Social Unrest & Political Instability – Poverty leads to protests, civil wars, and political instability.
Lack of Political Participation – Poor people often have less access to voting and decision-making processes.
D. Environmental Impacts
Slum Expansion – Overcrowding in urban slums leads to poor sanitation and higher disease rates.
Deforestation & Overfarming – To survive, poor communities exploit natural resources unsustainably.
Increased Waste & Pollution – Many poverty-stricken areas lack proper waste management, leading to pollution.
4. Discrimination and Poverty
Certain groups face higher poverty risks due to discrimination:
Women: Often paid less than men and denied education or job opportunities.
Ethnic Minorities & Indigenous Communities: Excluded from economic and social benefits.
Disabled Individuals: Struggle to find employment and access basic services.
Refugees & Migrants: Face difficulties in securing jobs, housing, and healthcare.
Discrimination limits access to education, healthcare, and financial opportunities, trapping marginalized groups in a cycle of poverty.
5. How is Poverty Measured?
Several indicators measure poverty levels in a country:
GDP Per Capita – The total economic output divided by the population; higher GDP per capita suggests lower poverty.
Human Development Index (HDI) – Measures education, life expectancy, and income levels.
Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) – Examines health, education, and living standards.
Gini Coefficient – Measures income inequality; the closer to 1, the greater the inequality.
Poverty Line – The minimum income required to afford basic needs (World Bank’s benchmark is $2.15/day).
6. How to Reduce Poverty?
A. Economic Strategies
Job Creation & Fair Wages – Governments and businesses must create employment opportunities with fair wages.
Microfinance & Entrepreneurship – Providing small loans can help poor individuals start businesses.
B. Social Strategies
Quality Education for All – Free and accessible education improves future employment chances.
Healthcare Access – Affordable medical services prevent financial crises due to medical expenses.
Women Empowerment – Equal pay, property rights, and education opportunities improve economic independence.
C. Political Strategies
Anti-Corruption Measures – Ensuring transparent governance helps fair wealth distribution.
Social Welfare Programs – Subsidies for food, housing, and healthcare can support low-income groups.
D. Environmental Strategies
Sustainable Agriculture – Improved farming techniques increase food production and income.
Disaster Preparedness & Climate Resilience – Protecting vulnerable communities from climate shocks can prevent sudden poverty.
Poverty is a complex, multi-dimensional issue that affects social, economic, political, and environmental aspects of life. Reducing poverty requires a combination of policies, education, healthcare access, fair economic opportunities, and environmental sustainability.
