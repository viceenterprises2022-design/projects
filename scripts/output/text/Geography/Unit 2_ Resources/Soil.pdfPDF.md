Introduction to soil health
Soil health is the capacity of soil to function as a vital living ecosystem that sustains its
functions. Soil is a complex environment with many components, including organic
matter, communities of micro-organisms (microbes), air and water pores, and
minerals.
Sadly, human activities can damage soil health impacting entire ecosystems. However,
some agricultural practices, such as regenerative farming, or integrated pest
management, can help protect and restore soil, so it can maintain its vital functions.
In this article we explore soil health, why it is important, how to improve it and future
strategies.
The functions of soil health
Healthy soil sustains essential functions such as:
•  Water regulation: stores water and regulates movement.
•  Support for plant and animal life: contributes to biodiversity and productivity of
living organisms.
•  Pollutant ﬁltering: minerals and soil microbes ﬁltrate pollutants, which protect
groundwater quality.
•  Nutrient cycling: soil microbes participate in the storage and cycling of nutrients
like phosphorus, nitrogen and carbon.
•  Physical stability and support: acts as a medium for plants to grow in and
support human construction.
Understanding the physical, chemical, and biological aspects of a healthy soil
Soil health is a combination of three main soil properties:
•  Physical properties: visual indicators, which include soil structure, aggregate
stability, crusting and compaction, permeability, texture, porosity, etc.
•  Chemical properties: they represent the chemical processes happening in the
soil, for instance, nutrient availability, pH, salinity, etc.
•  Biological properties: include visual indicators such as macro-organisms like
earthworms, collembola, mites, etc. and their activity and byproducts.
All these aspects interact dynamically and can foster healthy soil together. For instance,
soil structure inﬂuences water retention, which creates a good environment for
microorganisms.

Why is soil health important?
Soil is the foundation of agriculture and sustains life on earth by providing nutrients,
clean air and water. Healthy soil helps produce robust plants, increasing crop yields,
food production and food security. Heathy soil also stores carbon from the atmosphere,
mitigating climate change.
Unfortunately, soil health is under threat worldwide due to several drivers, including
intensive agriculture, deforestation, erosion, pollution, and improper fertilizer and
application. A recent study estimates that 61% of lands in the European Union are
aPected by soil degradation, while globally, the FAO reports that 33% of soils are
already degraded. In addition, the IPBES states that over 90% of soils could become
degraded by 2050.
The consequences of soil degradation and poor soil health go well beyond the loss of
good soil for farming. They aPect various aspects of human well-being and the
environment. For instance, as degraded soil holds less water, it can decrease the
amount of water available for plants and worsen drought.
What is soil degradation?
Soil degradation is the physical, chemical and biological decline in soil quality, which
can be caused by human activities or natural processes. Factors inﬂuencing soil
degradation include:
•  Soil compaction by heavy farm machinery, excessive tillage and leaving the soil
bare. Compacted soil does not have enough space for the movement of air,
water and plant roots.
•  Soil erosion or the loss of the top layer of soil at a rate faster than it is formed.
This layer is the fertile portion of soil and is essential for plant growth. Erosion is
mainly due to soil being exposed to climatic conditions after tillage and
deforestation. Soil erosion can also increase pollution in water bodies.
•  Chemical pollution from pesticides, fertilizers and other industrial chemicals.
This negatively impacts soil microbes, decreases the amount and diversity of soil
organisms, and reduces soil fertility.
•  Tillage that releases carbon into the atmosphere, contributing to the emissions
of greenhouse gases and causing poor health of soil. Overall, this contributes to
climate change.
As a result of soil degradation, soil quality gets poorer in nutrients and loses its capacity
to hold water. This causes food production to decrease; in the worst cases, degraded
soil cannot support plant growth anymore.
We must protect soil health at all costs so that soils can sustain its functions,
safeguarding the environment and human well-being.

How to improve soil health for gardening
You can promote soil health at home in your own garden by:
•  Creating compost from food waste, grass clipping or plant debris, for instance.
In doing so, you can avoid the overuse of chemical fertilizers.
•  Mulching increases the soil organic matter and promotes the activity of soil
microbes.
•  Using biological control are nature-based environmentally-friendly
products that protect your garden from pests and diseases and improve plant
health.

Agricultural practices to promote soil health
Whilst agriculture is a driver of soil health decline, there are several agricultural
practices that can reverse the trend and help restore soil health. Following these,
growers can:
•  Reduce the use of chemicals by opting for nature-based solutions
like biocontrol and bioprotection products, which have many advantages. These
natural products are safer for non-target organisms and preserve soil
biodiversity.
•
Increase the amount of organic matter going back into the ﬁelds with a cover
crop by leaving crop residues on the soil surface or using organic mulch.
•  Reduce soil compaction from machinery and livestock through minimum or
zero tillage and pasture management to avoid over-grazing. This minimizes soil
disturbance, which helps build back soil health.
•  Rotate crops to increase diversity above and within soil to break the cycle of
pests and diseases. Legumes are good crops for rotation as they increase
nitrogen in the soil.

The role of organic matter in soil health
Organic matter is any material produced originally by living organisms (plants and
animals) and is returned to the soil for further decomposition.
Rich soil with organic matter has biodiversity, like bacteria, viruses and fungi, but also
macro-organisms such as beneﬁcial insects or worms.
The presence of organic matter in soil is essential as a source of nutrients for macro-
and microbial organisms. These organisms break down nutrients that are absorbed by
plants.
The importance of organic matter and biodiversity in soil
Organic matter helps soil to function and inﬂuences it, directly and indirectly, in many
ways, such as:
•
Improving soil structure
•  Regulating soil pH, temperature and aeration
•  Promoting microbial activity and diversity
•  Supplies nutrients essential for plant growth
•  Enhancing disease suppression
•  Enhancing water absorption and retention
•  Reducing soil erosion, avoiding the loss of fertile lands.
Organic matter is therefore vital for enhancing overall soil health and fertility and
increasing soil biodiversity. Likely, soil biodiversity is essential for the cycling of
nutrients and water as well as pest suppression, all of which support plant growth and
health. In other words, soil biodiversity provides us with food and clean water.
The dynamics between organic matter and nutrient cycling
Diagram illustrating the nutrient cycle: organic matter is decomposed by
microorganisms, producing nutrients that can be assimilated by plants. © CABI
Organic matter is a reservoir of nutrients that are slowly released into the soil through its
decomposition over time. Microbial organisms decompose organic matter to break
down these essential nutrients into a form that plants can absorb. As a result, soil
fertility increases, and plants can grow better and stronger. Proper management of
organic matter ensures a balanced nutrient cycle. This will contribute to long-term crop
productivity and a balanced ecosystem.
From composting to mulching: Practical approaches to increase soil organic
matter
Several practical approaches increase soil organic matter in gardening and agricultural
systems. Two ways to do this are via:
•  Composting: once fully decomposed, compost becomes organic matter full of
nutrients that can return to the soil.
•  Mulching: by applying any material to the soil to cover it. Wood chips, shavings,
bark or plant residues can be used. As the mulch covers the soil surface, the soil
retains moisture more ePectively, weeds have more diPiculty germinating and
growing, and soil microbial ﬂora increases.
The impact of soil structure on water regulation and plant growth
Soil structure is a fundamental aspect of soil health, directly inﬂuencing its ability to
regulate water and support plant growth.
Soil structure refers to the aggregates of soil particles and pores (or spaces) between
them. A well-structured, healthy soil has large pore spaces that allow water to penetrate
the soil rather than run oP it, air to circulate better, and plant root to reach nutrients and
water more ePectively.
On the contrary, compacted or poorly structured soil restricts water movement and root
growth, leading to reduced plant growth. Over time, these factors contribute to soil
degradation, further diminishing soil health.
Soil health and climate change
Soil health and climate change are intricately linked. Climate change aPects soils
through extreme weather events, higher temperatures, or changes in precipitation
patterns, to name a few. These impact soil health in many ways. For instance:
•  Reduced amount of organic matter from a high rate of decomposition
•  Disturbed soil structure
•  Reduced soil moisture due to high temperatures
•
Increased erosion
Soil health also profoundly impacts climate. Healthy soil can absorb carbon from the
atmosphere (CO2) through carbon sequestration and store it in the soil, eventually
limiting greenhouse gas emissions. However, unhealthy soil contributes to the emission
of CO2 or methane into the atmosphere in several ways, including the loss of organic
matter. Unhealthy soil also sequesters less carbon, aggravating the impact of climate
change.
Therefore, good soil health is one vital part of helping tackle climate change.
The future of soil health: innovations and strategies
Given the importance of maintaining soil health, advances in technology give an
opportunity to utilize these new mediums to help monitor, record and protect soil.
Technological advances in soil health assessment and management
Additionally, precision agriculture and smart technologies are starting to transform how
farming systems are managed. In terms of soil health, tools such as soil sensors,
drones, and satellite imagery can collect lots of soil variables that can support farmers
in making informed decisions.
Measurable soil variables include, for instance, moisture levels, soil pH, and nutrient
content. Information from these measures can help adjust irrigation schedules,
optimize fertilizer application, and more. These technologies are not only improving
crop yields but also promoting soil conservation and long-term health.
US policy
A promising development is the recent introduction of US policy to protect soil health,
promoting sustainable agricultural practices to improve food security and combat
climate change. Policymakers are introducing laws to promote soil health at the federal
and state levels.
For instance, the USDA’s Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) has several
programmes that provide technical and ﬁnancial assistance to farmers and landowners
in implementing conservation practices that improve soil health.
The 2018 Farm Bill, extended in 2023, is one of the country’s most signiﬁcant funding in
the conservation and management of farmlands. It provides programmes that support
healthy soil practices.
Global movement towards soil health and sustainability
Soil health is increasingly recognized as a cornerstone of sustainability. There is a
growing movement towards prioritizing soil health through practices such as
regenerative agriculture, agroecology and climate-smart agriculture. Global initiatives,
such as the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the Global Soil
Partnership, emphasize the critical role of healthy soil in achieving food security and
tackling climate change.
Countries worldwide are adopting policies and practices that promote soil
conservation. Collaborative ePorts among governments, NGOs, and the private sector
are driving research, education, and the implementation of sustainable soil
management techniques, fostering a global commitment to preserving and enhancing
soil health.
By embracing these sustainable practices and fostering global cooperation, we can
ensure that our soil continues to support life, safeguard the environment, and provide
food security for generations to come.
