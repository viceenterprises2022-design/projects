Between 1347 and 1351, Europe faced one of the most devastating pandemics in human
history. The Black Death swept across the continent, killing an estimated 75-200 million
people and fundamentally altering medieval society. This catastrophic plague didn't
merely claim lives4it transformed economies, challenged religious beliefs, and reshaped
the very structure of European civilisation. Understanding this pandemic helps us grasp
how disease can become a powerful agent of historical change.

The Black Death began its deadly journey in Central Asia around 1346,
spreading rapidly along established trade networks. The bustling Silk
Road, which connected East and West, became an unwitting highway for
disaster. Merchants, soldiers, and travellers unknowingly carried infected
fleas and rats across vast distances.
The plague reached Europe in 1347 when Genoese trading ships arrived at
the Black Sea port of Caffa (modern-day Feodosia). These vessels carried
more than goods4they brought death itself. Within months, the disease
had spread to major Mediterranean ports and began its relentless march
across the continent.
The bacterium Yersinia pestis was the microscopic culprit, though
medieval people didn't understand its nature. It lived in fleas that infested
black rats, creating a deadly triangle of disease transmission that
medieval hygiene couldn't combat.

Medieval doctors operated under the theory of the four humours: blood,
phlegm, black bile, and yellow bile. They believed illness resulted from
imbalances between these bodily fluids. This fundamentally flawed
understanding led to treatments that were often harmful rather than
helpful.
01
Bloodletting
Doctors would cut veins or use leeches to "rebalance" humours, actually
weakening patients further.
02
Bursting Buboes
Painful lancing of swollen lymph nodes, often causing secondary
infections.
03
Herbal Remedies
Various plants and concoctions, some providing minor comfort but no
cure.
04
Supernatural Treatments
Trepanning (drilling skull holes) to release "evil spirits" was attempted in
desperate cases.
Some communities tried fumigating with vinegar and water, while others burned aromatic herbs. The famous plague doctor masks with long beaks were
stuffed with herbs, believing they would filter "bad air." Despite these efforts, medical ignorance meant the death toll continued to climb.
