Grade 7 Chemistry FA-1 (Criteria A & B)
Criterion A: Knowing & Understanding
Q1. Multiple Choice Questions                                                                        ( Level 1–2)
a) Correct answer: B. Copper → Element
b) Correct answer: C. They have a fixed composition.
Q2. Cutting Aluminium foil into tiny pieces turns it into a compound. Explain
why it is incorrect?                                                                                                   (Level 3–4)
Cutting Aluminium foil into tiny pieces does not turn it into a compound because it
is made of only one type of atom—Aluminium—so it remains an element. Cutting
only changes the size and shape; no new substance is formed. Each small piece
still consists of the same Aluminium atoms, so no compound is created.
Q3. Distinguish between a mixture and a compound by comparing air with
water (H₂O). Give two differences.                                                               (Level 3–4)
Composition: Mixture (air) has variable composition; Compound (H₂O) has fixed
composition.
Separation: Mixtures can be separated physically; Compounds need chemical methods.
Properties: Substances in a mixture retain properties; In a compound, properties are
different from elements.
Q4. Silver solid forming black layer (Classify) (Level 1–2)
Silver solid = Element
Black layer (silver oxide) = Compound
Criterion B: Inquiring & Designing
a. Research Question (Level 1-2)
“How does the length (or mass) of magnesium ribbon affect the mass of magnesium oxide
formed?”
b. Hypothesis with Explanation (Level 3–4)
Hypothesis:
If the length of magnesium ribbon is increased, then the mass of magnesium oxide formed
will also increase, because more magnesium will be available to react with oxygen to
produce more ash (magnesium oxide).
c. Variables (Level 3–4)
Independent Variable (IV): Length (or mass) of magnesium ribbon.
Dependent Variable (DV): Mass of magnesium oxide formed.
Controlled Variables (CV): Type of flame, crucible, lid, balance, same type of
ribbon, environment.
d. Safety Precaution (Level 1–2)
Any one valid precaution:
Wear safety goggles to protect your eyes from bright light and ash.
Use tongs to hold hot crucible.
Avoid looking directly at burning magnesium.

6. Isotopes: Scientific Tool or Global Risk?
Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have different numbers of neutrons. Their unique
properties allow scientists to use them  in  medicine, environmental studies, archaeology, and
energy research. In hospitals, radioactive isotopes such as  Technetium-99m help physicians
create  internal  images  of  the  body  without  surgery.  When  a  patient  is  given  a  substance
containing an isotope like Technetium-99m, it travels to specific organs or tissues. This isotope
emits gamma rays, which specialized cameras can detect. This allows doctors to create detailed,
non-surgical images of the body's internal functions (e.g., blood flow, organ size, and potential
diseases), helping them diagnose problems earlier. In archaeology, Carbon-14 dating allows
researchers to estimate the age of fossils and ancient artefacts. All living things absorb a small
amount of Carbon-14 from the atmosphere. When an organism dies, the Carbon-14 starts to
decay at a known, constant rate (its half-life). By measuring how much Carbon-14 remains in
an  ancient  bone or  piece  of  wood,  scientists  can  calculate  how  long  ago the organism  died,
providing  a  timeline  for  history.  Scientists  can  use  both  stable  and  radioactive  isotopes  to
follow materials through the environment. For example, by introducing a specific isotope into
a field, agricultural researchers can track exactly how a plant absorbs nutrients from the soil,
helping  farmers  use  fertilizers  more  efficiently.  Environmental  scientists  can  track  where  a
chemical spill originated or where it is spreading using these unique "isotopic signatures."
However, the growing use of isotopes raises concerns. Radioactive isotopes used in hospitals
or  produced  in  nuclear  facilities  create  waste  that  remains  hazardous  for  long  periods—
sometimes  hundreds  or  thousands  of  years.  This  waste  must  be  securely  stored  in  special,
shielded facilities deep underground or far from populations to prevent contamination of soil
and water. The processes of mining uranium (a source material) and producing isotopes also
require strict safety protocols to protect workers' health and local ecosystems from radiation
exposure.  The  use  of  advanced  isotopic  technology  requires  sophisticated  infrastructure,
trained personnel, and effective monitoring systems to be safe. There are ethical concerns about
deploying  these  materials  in  regions  or  countries  with  poor  healthcare  infrastructure,  weak
regulatory systems, or limited resources to handle a potential accident or safely manage waste.
The risk of misuse or accidental exposure is higher in these scenarios, raising questions about
global equity and responsible technology transfer.

can be weighed.

and chemical properties than the original elements.
Procedure:
1.  Collect the following materials: magnesium ribbon, a Bunsen burner, a crucible and
lid, a weighing scale, a test tube, a delivery tube, and a water trough.
2.  Cut a small piece of magnesium ribbon and weigh it using the scale. Record its mass.
3.  Place the magnesium ribbon inside the crucible and cover it with the lid.
4.  Connect the delivery tube to the test tube and place the test tube in the water trough.
5.  Heat the crucible using the Bunsen burner until the magnesium ribbon reacts with the
oxygen in the air, producing magnesium oxide.
6.  Observe the test tube and record any changes in the water level, indicating the release
of gas.
7.  Allow the crucible to cool and weigh it again, along with the magnesium oxide inside.
Record the mass.
8.  Calculate the mass of the magnesium oxide formed and compare it to the initial mass
of the magnesium ribbon.
9.  Examine the properties of the magnesium oxide, such as its color, melting point, and
solubility, and compare them to the properties of magnesium and oxygen.

•  Before the reaction: a shiny, silvery piece of magnesium ribbon
•  During the reaction: the magnesium ribbon glows brightly and a white powder forms

•  After the reaction: a white powder (magnesium oxide) remains in the crucible

magnesium  oxide,  which  has  different  properties  than  the  original  elements.  Magnesium
oxide is a white solid with a high melting point and is insoluble in water, whereas magnesium
is a shiny, silvery metal that is relatively soft and easily oxidized, and oxygen is a colorless,
odorless gas that supports combustion. The amount of magnesium used in the experiment
affected  the  amount  of  magnesium  oxide  produced,  but  the  properties  of  the  magnesium
oxide were consistent regardless of the amount used.

Topic: Isotopes and Their Uses
1. What Are Isotopes?
Definition: Isotopes are atoms of the same chemical element that have the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons in their nucleus.
Atomic Number vs. Mass Number:
The number of protons (the atomic number) determines which element it is (e.g., all Carbon atoms have 6 protons).
The total number of protons and neutrons is the mass number, which is different for each isotope.
Example: Carbon has three main isotopes:
Carbon-12: 6 protons, 6 neutrons (stable)
Carbon-13: 6 protons, 7 neutrons (stable)
Carbon-14: 6 protons, 8 neutrons (radioactive)
2. Radioactive vs. Non-Radioactive Isotopes
3. Uses of Isotopes
Isotopes, especially radioactive ones (radioisotopes), have many practical applications because their radiation can be detected or used to change things.
A. Medicine
Isotopes are used for both diagnosis (finding problems) and therapy (treating diseases).
Diagnosis (Tracers): Small amounts of short-lived radioactive isotopes (tracers) are put into the body (injection, swallowing, or breathing). A special camera detects the radiation to create images of organs and check how they are working.
Technetium-99m: The most common isotope used to image bones, heart, liver, and many other organs.
Iodine-131/123: Used to check how the thyroid gland is functioning.
Treatment (Therapy): Higher doses of radiation are used to kill diseased cells.
Cobalt-60: Used in radiotherapy machines to focus gamma rays on cancer tumours to kill the cancer cells.
Iodine-131: Used in large doses to treat thyroid cancer by being absorbed directly by the thyroid gland.
B. Archaeology (Carbon Dating)
Dating Ancient Objects: The radioactive isotope Carbon-14 (C-14) is used to determine the age of dead plants, animals, and ancient artifacts (like wooden tools or fossils) that were once alive.
How it Works: All living things absorb Carbon-14 from the atmosphere. When they die, they stop absorbing it. The C-14 then decays at a known, constant rate (its half-life is about 5,730 years). By measuring how much  C-14 is left, scientists can estimate how long ago the organism died.
C. Food Industry
Food Preservation (Irradiation): Food products (like fruits, vegetables, and meats) are exposed to gamma radiation from sources like Cobalt-60.
Benefits: This radiation kills bacteria (like Salmonella and E. coli), insects, and microorganisms, which helps prevent food poisoning and makes the food last longer (extends shelf life). Irradiating food does not make it radioactive.
D. Agriculture
Plant Nutrition Research: Scientists use stable and radioactive isotopes (like Nitrogen-15 or Phosphorus-32) as tracers in fertilisers. They track how much fertiliser a plant absorbs and determine the most effective amount and timing to use, which helps farmers save money and reduces pollution.
Pest Control: Radiation is used to sterilise male insect pests in labs. These sterile males are released into the wild to mate with females, which results in no offspring, helping to control pest populations naturally (Sterile Insect Technique).
Crop Improvement: Exposing seeds to controlled radiation can cause useful genetic changes (mutations) to create new, hardier types of crops that resist disease or grow better.

Thomson’s Atomic Model: Plum Pudding Model of Atom
In this chapter, you will understand Thomson’s atomic model, also known as plum pudding model of atom and its drawbacks or limitations. After the discovery of sub-atomic particles, such as electron and proton, the scientists were faced several major challenges:
to understand the stability of atom.
to compare the behaviour of elements based on their physical and chemical properties.
to explain the formation of different kinds of molecules by the combination of different atoms.
to understand the origin and nature of the characteristics of electromagnetic radiation absorbed or emitted by atoms.
Scientists proposed various atomic models to explain the arrangement and distribution of charged particles (electrons and protons) within an atom. While some of these models failed to explain the stability of atoms, two notable models that made significant contributions were:
J.J. Thomson’s Atomic Model (1904)
Ernest Rutherford’s Model (1911)
J. J. Thomson’s Atomic Model
After the discovery of electrons and protons within an atom, the next question was to know how these particles are arranged in an atom. In 1898, J. J. Thomson was proposed the first scientific model to explain the internal structure of an atom. This atomic model is also popularly known as plum pudding model of the atom. He proposed his model of an atom before the discovery of the neutron.
J. J. Thomson proposed that an atom consists of a uniform sphere, with a radius of approximately 10-10 m or 10-8 cm in which the positive charge is uniformly distributed over the entire sphere. The negatively charged particles (electrons) are embedded within this sphere in such a way that they create the most stable electrostatic arrangement.
At equilibrium, the total positive charge inside the atom is equal to the total negative charge possessed by the electrons. As a result, the net charge in the system is zero, making every atom is electrically neutral.
Positive charge = negative charge (at equilibrium)
An important feature of Thomson’s atomic model is that the mass of the atom is considered to be uniformly distributed throughout the atom. This model of atom has given many different names, such as plum pudding model, or raisin pudding model or watermelon model. It successfully described the phenomenon of thermionic emission, photoelectric emission, and ionization.
Why Is Thomson’s Atomic Model Called Plum Pudding Model?
J. J. Thomson’s model is called the “plum pudding model” because he compared the atom to a British Christmas cake known as plum pudding. In this model, the atom is visualized as a soft, round pudding that represents the positive charge, and electrons like small plums scattered throughout over the pudding.
According to the plum pudding model, the positive charge and the electrons are mixed together inside the atom, similar to how plums are mixed into a pudding. Hence, the plum pudding model of the atom describes an atom as a sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded within it, much like plums into pudding.
Drawbacks or Limitations of Thomson’s Model of Atom
Although this atomic model was able to explain the overall neutrality of the atom, but over the development of new concepts, this model was discarded because of the following reasons:
This model failed to explain the origin of the spectral lines in the form of series observed in atomic spectra. For example, hydrogen atom. Niels Bohr’s model and quantum mechanics explained it later.
It failed to explain the scattering of alpha particles through the large angles in the Ernest Rutherford’s gold foil experiment in 1909.
Thomson’s atomic model was an earlier step in the development of atomic theory because it introduced the concept of the electron. He was awarded by Nobel Prize for physics in 1906, for his theoretical and experimental researches on the conduction of electricity by gases.

Case Study: The Fascinating World of Transition Elements
Imagine you're walking into a chemistry lab, where you notice a series of shiny metals and colorful liquids. Some of them look like jewelry, while others are used to make important tools or medicines. What’s fascinating about these metals? These metals are called transition elements, and they are special in many ways! Today, we are going to explore their exciting properties—like how they change color and work as catalysts—and how this makes them useful in our daily lives. We will look closely at four metals: Iron (Fe), Silver (Ag), Gold (Au), and Mercury (Hg).
What Are Transition Elements?
Transition elements are metals found in the middle of the periodic table. They have unique properties that make them stand out from other metals. Some of their special traits include:
Catalytic Properties: Many transition metals are used as catalysts. Catalysts help speed up chemical reactions.
Colorful Compounds: When transition metals form compounds with other elements, these compounds often have bright, beautiful colors. Transition metal compounds show color because their electrons absorb certain colors of light and reflect others. The color we see is the light that is not absorbed by the electrons.
Iron (Fe):
Iron is one of the most important metals in the world, and it is used to make steel, which is an alloy found in buildings, cars, and bridges. Iron is also an amazing catalyst. In the process of making ammonia, used for fertilisers, iron is used as a catalyst. Iron also has colorful compounds, like rust (iron oxide), which is reddish-brown. But did you know that iron also plays a role in biology? Hemoglobin in our blood uses iron to carry oxygen. Without it, we wouldn't be able to breathe properly!
Curiosity Question: Why do you think iron is used in such important industries like building construction and car manufacturing?
Silver (Ag):
Silver is a shiny, precious metal known for its use in jewelry and coins. But did you know silver also has the amazing ability to act as a catalyst? Silver speeds up reactions in some important processes, like in the production of formaldehyde, which is used in disinfectants. What’s even more interesting is that silver compounds, like silver nitrate, are used in photography. When exposed to light, these compounds change color, forming black-and-white images!
Curiosity Question: How do you think silver's ability to change color can be used to create photographs?
Gold (Au): The Inert Metal with Amazing Uses
Gold is one of the most famous transition elements because of its beauty and rarity. People have used gold for coins and jewelry for thousands of years. But while gold is known for being shiny, it is also very stable and doesn’t react easily with other elements. This makes it perfect for electronics! In smartphones and computers, gold is used to make connections because it doesn't corrode over time. Gold is also a catalyst in some reactions, especially in the production of cleaner fuels.
Curiosity Question: Why do you think gold is used in electronics, even though it's much more expensive than other metals?
Mercury (Hg): The Liquid Metal
Mercury is one of the only metals that is liquid at room temperature. It’s used in thermometers, barometers, and even in some electrical devices. Mercury is also an important catalyst in the production of chlorine and sodium hydroxide, which are used to make many household products, like bleach and soap. However, mercury compounds are very toxic, so it must be handled with care.
Curiosity Question: Why do you think mercury is used in thermometers, even though it can be dangerous?
Conclusion: The Special World of Transition Metals
Transition elements are like the superheroes of the periodic table. Their unique properties help make our world a better place. From speeding up reactions to making beautiful jewelry, these metals are all around us, working in ways we often don’t even notice. So, the next time you see a shiny piece of silver jewelry or a sturdy steel bridge, remember that transition elements are behind the scenes, making it all possible!

Why do you think scientists choose to use transition metals like iron and silver in certain reactions?
How does the color of a transition metal compound tell us about the metal’s properties?

1. Explain how Mendeleev’s periodic table helped countries discover new elements and develop useful materials. (Economic impact)
Mendeleev organized elements based on their atomic mass and similar chemical properties. When he created his periodic table, he left empty spaces for elements that had not been discovered yet. He also predicted the properties of these missing elements.
Later, scientists discovered elements such as gallium and germanium, and their properties were very similar to what Mendeleev predicted. This helped scientists trust the periodic table and use it as a tool to guide discoveries.
As new elements and materials were discovered, countries were able to develop new technologies and industries. For example, some elements are used in electronics, batteries, and construction materials, which helps economic growth and technological development
2. Discuss how the modern periodic table helps scientists solve environmental problems such as pollution or saving natural resources. (Environmental impact)
The modern periodic table organizes elements according to their atomic number and chemical properties. This helps scientists understand how different elements react with each other.
Scientists use this knowledge to develop solutions for environmental problems. For example, some elements are used in catalysts that reduce pollution in car exhaust systems. Other elements help create renewable energy technologies, such as solar panels and batteries.
The periodic table also helps scientists identify safer materials and ways to recycle elements, which helps conserve natural resources. By understanding the properties of elements, scientists can design more environmentally friendly technologies.
3. Compare and contrast the two periodic tables and explain how their development has helped us.
Mendeleev’s periodic table arranged elements according to their atomic mass, while the modern periodic table arranges elements according to their atomic number (number of protons).
One similarity is that both tables group elements with similar properties together. This helps scientists understand patterns in chemical behavior.
However, the modern periodic table is more accurate and complete because it includes more elements and correct placement based on atomic number. It also includes new groups such as noble gases that were discovered later.
The development of the periodic table has helped scientists predict properties of elements, discover new materials, and improve technology. Today, it is an important tool used in science, medicine, and environmental research.

Electron arrangement
An electron arrangement is the way in which electrons are arranged in an atom.
Electrons in shells
Electrons occupy different energy levels, or shells. Each shell can hold a maximum number of electrons. Moving through the elements in the periodic table, each atom has one more electron than the last because the number of electrons is the same as the atomic number. Electrons occupy the shells in order, starting with the shell that is nearest the nucleus. They begin to occupy the next shell only when this shell becomes full.
For elements with atomic number 1 to 20:
Predicting an electron arrangement
The electron arrangement of an atom can be worked out from its atomic number. For example, the atomic number of sodium is 11. Sodium atoms have 11 protons and so 11 electrons.
2 electrons occupy the first shell
8 electrons occupy the second shell
1 electron occupies the third shell
This electron arrangement can be written as 2.8.1 (each dot separates one shell from the next). It can also be shown as a diagram. In these diagrams:
each shell is modelled as a circle
each electron is modelled as a dot or a cross
Figure caption,The electron arrangement of sodium as a diagram
Electron arrangements and the periodic table
The electron arrangement of an element is related to its position on the periodic table.
The electron arrangement of sodium (2.8.1) shows that sodium, Na:
is in period 3
is in group 1
has an atomic number of (2 + 8 + 1) = 11
The number of electron shells is therefore the same across a period but increases when moving down a group.
Electron arrangements and properties of elements
The electron arrangements of atoms help explain the properties of elements and the structure of the periodic table. When substances react, it is only the outer electrons in the atoms that are involved - the nature of the reactions which occur depend upon the number of outer shell electrons in the reacting atoms.
So, elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons in their outer shell.

Binary ionic compounds are made of:

Steps (Criss-Cross Method):
Write the symbols of the ions.
Write their charges.
Criss-cross the charges to become subscripts.
Remove charges and simplify if needed.

Magnesium ion: Mg²⁺
Chloride ion: Cl⁻
Criss-cross → Mg₁Cl₂
Correct formula: MgCl₂
Example 2: Aluminium oxide
Aluminium ion: Al³⁺
Oxide ion: O²⁻
Criss-cross → Al₂O₃
Correct formula: Al₂O₃

Ca²⁺ and O²⁻

Law of Conservation of Matter
What is the Law of Conservation of Matter?
The Law of Conservation of Matter states that:
Matter cannot be created or destroyed during a physical or chemical change.
This means:
The total mass before a reaction is equal to the total mass after the reaction
atoms are rearranged, but they do not disappear
Matter and Atoms
Everything around us is made of matter.
Matter is made up of tiny particles called atoms.
During changes:
atoms may separate
atoms may join together differently
atoms are never lost
Physical Changes and Conservation of Matter
A physical change changes only the form or state of matter.
Examples:
melting ice
boiling water
tearing paper
The particles remain the same substance.
Example: Melting Ice
Ice changes into liquid water.
The substance remains water, so matter is conserved.
Chemical Changes and Conservation of Matter
A chemical change forms new substances.
Examples:
rusting iron
burning wood
cooking food
Atoms rearrange to form new compounds.
Example of a Chemical Reaction
Hydrogen reacts with oxygen to form water.
Count the atoms:
The number of atoms is equal on both sides.
Why Chemical Equations Must Be Balanced
Balanced equations show that:
matter is conserved
atoms are neither created nor destroyed
reactions follow scientific laws correctly
Everyday Examples of Conservation of Matter
Burning a Candle
The wax changes into gases like carbon dioxide and water vapor.
Matter is not lost; it changes form.
Rusting Iron
Iron reacts with oxygen from the air to form rust.
The atoms are rearranged into a new substance called iron oxide.
Key Vocabulary
Important Points to Remember
Matter cannot disappear.
Atoms rearrange during reactions.
Balanced equations follow the law of conservation of matter.
Mass remains constant in a closed system.
Rusting of an Iron Bridge
A large iron bridge near a coastal city was exposed to moist air and rainwater for many years. Engineers noticed reddish-brown rust forming on the surface of the bridge. Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water in the environment.
The chemical reaction is:
During this process:
iron atoms combine with oxygen atoms
no atoms are destroyed
the total mass of matter remains conserved
Although the bridge appeared to gain mass because oxygen was added to the iron, the total amount of matter in the system stayed the same.
Engineers painted the bridge with protective coatings to reduce rusting and increase safety.
Real-Life Importance
Understanding conservation of matter helps scientists and engineers:
design safer buildings and bridges
reduce corrosion damage
calculate amounts of chemicals in industries
protect materials from environmental changes

Step-wise method to balance a chemical equation
Example (unbalanced): H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
Step 1: Write the correct formula
Make sure each substance is written correctly:
Hydrogen = H₂, Oxygen = O₂, Water = H₂O.
Step 2: Count atoms on both sides
Oxygen is not balanced.
Step 3: Balance the element that is not balanced (oxygen)
To make oxygen equal, put 2 in front of water:
H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Now count again:
Oxygen: Left 2, Right 2 ✅
Hydrogen: Left 2, Right 4 ❌ (not balanced now)
Step 4: Balance hydrogen
Put 2 in front of H₂:
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Step 5: Check again (final check)
✅ Balanced equation: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Key rules students must remember
Never change the small subscripts in a formula (e.g., do not change H₂O to H₂O₂).
Only add coefficients in front (numbers like 2, 3, 4).
Balance one element at a time, then recheck at the end.
1. Reactions of Alkali Metals with Water
Alkali metals (such as lithium, sodium, and potassium) are highly reactive.
They react with water to form:
A metal hydroxide
Hydrogen gas
Heat energy (exothermic reaction)
Metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen
Example: Sodium reacts vigorously with water. Hydrogen gas is released, and the solution becomes alkaline due to sodium hydroxide.
Word equation: Sodium + Water → Sodium hydroxide + Hydrogen
Balanced equation: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂
2. Reactions of Alkali Metals with Oxygen
Alkali metals also react with oxygen in air to form metal oxides.
Example: Lithium combines with oxygen to form lithium oxide. This is a synthesis (combination) reaction.
Word equation:Lithium + Oxygen → Lithium oxide
Balanced equation: 4Li + O₂ → 2Li₂O
3. Reactions of Halogens with Metals
Halogens (such as chlorine and bromine) react with metals to form metal halides.
These reactions are usually fast and form ionic compounds.
Example: Sodium loses an electron and chlorine gains an electron, forming the compound sodium chloride (common salt).
Word equation: Sodium + Chlorine → Sodium chloride
Balanced equation: 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl
4. Reactions of Halogens with Hydrogen
Halogens also react with hydrogen gas to form hydrogen halides.
Example: Hydrogen and chlorine combine to form hydrogen chloride gas. When dissolved in water, it forms hydrochloric acid.
Word equation: Hydrogen + Chlorine → Hydrogen chloride
Balanced equation: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl

Types of chemical Reactions
1. Synthesis (Combination) Reaction
A synthesis reaction is a reaction in which two or more substances combine to form one
new substance.
Key idea: Many → One
Example: When iron reacts with sulfur, a new compound iron sulfide is formed.
Word Equation:       Iron + Sulphur → Iron sulphide
Balanced Equation:  Fe + S → FeS
2. Decomposition Reaction
A decomposition reaction is a reaction in which one compound breaks down into two or
more simpler substances.
Key idea: One → Many
(Usually needs heat, light, or electricity)
Example:
Calcium carbonate breaks down when heated to form Calcium oxide and carbondioxide
Word Equation:       Calcium carbonate → Calcium Oxide + Carbon dioxide
Balanced Equation:                CaCO₃ →  CaO + CO₂
3. Single Displacement Reaction
A single displacement reaction occurs when one element replaces another element in a
compound.
Key idea: One element replaces another. The more reactive metal displaces the less reactive
metal from its salt solution.
Example:
Zinc replaces copper from copper sulfate solution to form Zinc sulphate and copper
Word Equation: Zinc + copper sulfate → Zinc sulphate + copper
Balanced Equation:Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
4. Double Displacement Reaction
A double displacement reaction occurs when two compounds exchange ions to form two
new compounds.
Key idea: Exchange of partners
Example:
Sodium chloride reacts with silver nitrate to form solid silver chloride(white precipitate) and
sodium nitrate
Word Equation: Sodium Chloride + Silver Nitrate → Silver chloride + sodium nitrate
Balanced Equation: NaCl + AgNO₃ → AgCl + NaNO₃
5. Combustion Reaction
A combustion reaction occurs when a substance reacts with oxygen, producing heat and
light.
Key idea: Fuel + Oxygen → Energy
Example:
Burning magnesium ribbon in air forms magnesium oxide producing dazzling white light
with heat
Word Equation: Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium Oxide + heat
Balanced Equation: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
6. Exothermic Reaction
An exothermic reaction is a reaction that releases heat energy. Heat is released to the
surroundings, making the reaction feel warm.
Example:
Combustion of methane produces carbon dioxide and water
Word Equation: Methane + Oxygen → carbon dioxide + water+ heat
Balanced Equation: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + energy
7. Endothermic Reaction
An endothermic reaction is a reaction that absorbs heat energy from its surroundings. Heat
is needed for the reaction to occur.
Example:Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate. Calcium carbonate decomposes on
heating to form calcium oxide and carbon dioxide
Word Equation: Calcium carbonate + heat → Calcium oxide + carbon dioxide
Balanced Equation: CaCO₃ + heat → CaO + CO₂

szValency and Polyatomic Ions
1. What is Valency?
Valency is the combining capacity of an atom.
It tells us how many electrons an atom can lose, gain, or share when it forms a compound.
Atoms are made of electrons arranged in shells around the nucleus.
The outermost shell is called the valence shell. The number of electrons in this shell decides the valency of an atom.
If an atom has 1, 2, or 3 electrons in its outer shell, it usually loses them.
If an atom has 5, 6, or 7 electrons, it usually gains or shares electrons.
Atoms with 8 electrons in the outer shell are stable and usually do not react.
Examples:
Neon (Ne) has 8 electrons → Valency = 0
2. Role of Valency in Forming Compounds
Atoms form compounds to achieve a stable electron arrangement.
Valency helps us understand:
Which atoms can combine?
How many atoms combine?
The chemical formula of compounds
For example:
Magnesium (valency 2) combines with oxygen (valency 2) to form MgO(ionic)
Hydrogen (valency 1) combines with oxygen (valency 2) to form H₂O(covalent)
Valency acts like a rule that atoms follow when bonding.
List of some simple ions are given in the table below.
3. What are Polyatomic Ions?
A polyatomic ion is a group of two or more atoms that are chemically bonded and carry an overall charge.
These ions behave like a single unit in chemical reactions. Some common polyatomic ions are listed in the table above
4. Charge and Structure of Polyatomic Ions
The charge of a polyatomic ion comes from gaining or losing electrons
The atoms inside the ion are held together by strong bonds
The ion as a whole carries a positive or negative charge
Examples:
NH₄⁺ (Ammonium): One nitrogen atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms with an overall +1 charge
SO₄²⁻ (Sulfate): One sulfur atom bonded to four oxygen atoms with an overall –2 charge
CO₃²⁻ (Carbonate): One carbon atom bonded to three oxygen atoms with an overall –2 charge
5. Why are Polyatomic Ions Important?
Polyatomic ions are found in:
Fertilisers (ammonium, nitrate)
Soaps and detergents (carbonate)
Natural minerals and rocks
Biological systems (inside our body)
Understanding valency and polyatomic ions helps us:
Write correct chemical formulae
Understand how compounds are formed
Explain many chemical reactions around us

Writing Chemical Formulae and Naming of Compounds
Lesson Goals and ATL Skills
Master writing correct chemical formulae using valency (combining power), polyatomic
(group) ions, and Roman numerals for variable charge metals. This builds critical thinking
by  applying  the  "cross  method"  to  ensure  electrical  neutrality  (total  positive  charge
equals total negative charge).
Part 1: Formulae from Compound Names
Compound names reveal elements and atom ratios. Covalent compounds use Greek
preﬁxes; ionic compounds use valency for charge balance (no preﬁxes).
Covalent Examples:
•  Carbon monoxide: mono- (1 O) → CO
•  Carbon dioxide: di- (2 O) → CO₂
•  Nitrogen trichloride: tri- (3 Cl) → NCl₃
•  Dinitrogen tetroxide: di- (2 N), tetra- (4 O) → N₂O₄
•  Dinitrogen pentoxide: di- (2 N), penta- (5 O) → N₂O₅
Ionic Examples:
•  Sodium chloride: Na⁺ (1), Cl⁻ (1) → NaCl
•  Magnesium bromide: Mg²⁺ (2), Br⁻ (1) → MgBr₂
•  Potassium oxide: K⁺ (1), O²⁻ (2) → K₂O
Pitfalls: Confusing preﬁxes (e.g., CO₂ as monoxide); ignoring charge balance in ionic
(e.g., MgBr).

Metals lose (positive ions); non-metals gain (negative ions).

Cross Method Steps:
1.  Write symbols with valencies (ignore signs initially).
2.  Cross valencies as subscripts.
3.  Simplify ratios by dividing by the greatest common divisor.
4.  Drop 1's; metal ﬁrst.
Examples:
•  Sodium + Oxygen: Na (1) × O (2) → Na₂O
•  Calcium + Chlorine: Ca (2) × Cl (1) → CaCl₂
•  Aluminium + Nitrogen: Al (3) × N (3) → AlN (simplify 3:3)
•  Magnesium + Oxygen: Mg (2) × O (2) → Mg₂O₂ → MgO
Pitfalls: Forgetting to simplify (Mg₂O₂); adding charges in ﬁnal formula.
