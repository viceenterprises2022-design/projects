Q: What is an argument?
An argument is a statement that expresses an opinion and gives reasons or evidence to support it. (Argument = opinion + reason)
Q: What is a counter-argument?
A counter-argument is a statement that challenges or opposes the main argument. (Counter-argument = opinion + refutation of the argument’s reason)
Q: Why is it important to learn to write counter-arguments?
We use counter-arguments in debates and dissertations for research purposes. In debates, counter-arguments help us question and persuade others. In dissertations, they help clarify our concepts and make our writing clearer and more objective.
Q: How do we frame a counter-argument?
Step 1: Acknowledge the argument by explaining the connection between the opinion and the reason.
Step 2: Question or challenge the reason with new evidence or research, or knowledge from your own experience. This becomes your refutation.
Step 3: Conclude the counter-argument by restating the refutation and use it to frame your opinion based on your reason that is opposed to the main argument.

ADVERBS
Part 1
What are Adverbs?
An adverb is a word that tells us more about a verb, an adjective, or another adverb.
It adds more meaning to the verb (a verb is an action word. Used to describe action).
The best way to remember what an adverb does is to understand that it adds meaning to a
verb = add + verb → adverb.
It can answer questions like:
-  How was the action done? (manner) → quietly, slowly, together
Example: She spoke slowly
-  When did the action take place? (time) → today, now, then
Example: Today, we decide.
-  Where did the action take place? (place) → here, outside, up
Example: I will eat here.
-  How much action took place? / To what extent did the action take place? (degree) →
almost, very, just
I almost believed him.
-  Why did the action take place? / For what purpose did the action take place?
(reason/purpose) → so (that), therefore
Therefore, I am surprised.
The exam is so you may test yourself.
-
In what order did the action(s) take place? (sequence) → first, then, next
First, I would like to say a few words in favour of the exam. Next, we can debate the
method of evaluation.
-
In which way did the action not take place (Negation) → not, never
I thought you would never ask.
I did not trust him.
I do not wish to see you again.
Now that you have read up on adverbs, let’s spot adverbs. Underline the adverbs in the
following sentences. (Hint: First, spot the verbs – the action words. Then ask the verb as to
how the action is taking place).
1.  I arrived early this morning. (Hint: How did you arrive this morning? Answer: early)
2.  He goes about his day happily. (Hint: How does he go about his day?)
3.  I think she plays badly. (Hint: How does she play?)
Alert: Don’t assume all adverbs end in an ‘-ly’. A lot of them don’t. See below:
1.  He does very little for me. (Hint: How much does he do?)
2.  Mason writes well. (Hint: How does he write?)
3.  Thus, we agreed to disagree. (Hint: How did we come to agree?)
4.  How nice was that coffee – I’d say very. (Hint: how nice was the coffee?)
The thing to understand here is that adverbs are to verbs what adjectives are to nouns.
Adverbs add meaning to verbs. They tell us how somebody does something.
While,
Adjectives add meaning to nouns. They tell us how something or someone is.
It may be interesting to note that adverbs are oftentimes formed from adjectives. Let’s take a
few examples:
Example #1
Adjective: Joan of Arc was brave. Here, Joan is the name of a person. It’s a proper noun.
Brave is an adjective, which tells us more about the person. Here, brave modifies (adds
meaning to) the noun – Joan. You ask the noun: How was Joan of Arc? Brave. That’s an
adjective.
Adverb: Joan of Arc lived bravely. Here, bravely modifies the verb – the action that Joan of
Arc committed – that of living. Here, you ask the verb: How did Joan of Arc live? Bravely,
you get the answer. That’s an adverb.
Example #2
Adjective: My parents had decided I was going to boarding school and I was happy.
Adverb: Following my parents’ decision, I was happily on my way to boarding school.
Example #3
Adjective: My heart was heavy after I had argued with mom.
Adverb: My heart felt heavy after that argument with mom.

What was the American Civil War?
The American Civil War was fought in the United States between 1861 and 1865.
It was a war between:
The Union (Northern states, led by the U.S. federal government)
The Confederacy (Southern states that seceded and formed the Confederate States of America)
Why Did the War Begin?
Main Causes
Slavery – The most important issue.
Southern states depended on enslaved labor for agriculture (especially cotton). Many Northern states opposed the expansion of slavery into new territories.
States’ Rights – Southern states argued that individual states had the right to decide their own laws, including whether to allow slavery.
Economic Differences –
North: Industrial, manufacturing-based economy

Structure, Style and Literary Devices
Hendricks utilizes the structure of this poem not only to tell a story but to show movement. This poem is free verse and in a prose poetry style. Each stanza is either a different action/thought or adds information to the previous action/thought that provides the reader with a sense of progression. Because of the way Hendricks separated the lines and the content into stanzas, we almost feel as if we're moving along with the narrator's thoughts, moving along with the migrant. We can easily see the connections between stanzas.
The language adds to that sense of story and fits in perfectly with the prose poetry style Hendricks used. The larger words, like "realization" and "piteous," make us feel as if we're reading a more advanced short story while the phrases help us clearly imagine the thoughts and the imagery. Hendricks' choice of words and style leaves little open for interpretation in this point—a trait that can be quite common in prose poetry, as there's often more description and less words/points omitted. The tone of this language is very matter-of-fact, suggesting that no points of the story can be argued with and that everything occurring is just how things are supposed to be for these characters.
The imagery is largely used to create the setting. We know that the migrant and narrator are traveling, in some kind of terminal or station, and waiting in line for a door that says "Embarking Passengers Only." Consider, though, the imagery that Hendricks doesn't include. We don't get a clear image of the migrant, not being able to put an identifiable face to a body. This tactic makes it a little bit more easier for us, in our minds, to see ourselves at the migrant and imagine what she's going through. There's no hair color or eye color or clothing described to make us stop from seeing ourselves in this story. We also don't get a clear image of what type of traveling she's doing, omitting from our minds the potential conditions that could lie ahead for it. It helps us focus on the present and the character in front of us.
The travel brochures the migrant reads are the main form of symbolism in this poem. They suggest a monotonous existence, with nothing better to do than succumb to what lies ahead and read about it in a small brochure. The name of the gate at the end, "Embarking Passengers Only," also symbolizes finality. Once the migrants step through the gate, there's no turning back.
Language: The language is formal, businesslike, and intellectual--a style that is unusual for poetry yet consistent with the bureaucratic feel of having to move to another country. Words like transit, realization, departure, appreciably, eventually, and considering are intellectual rather than emotional words.
Tone: This formal language produces a somewhat detached although sympathetic tone. The woman described is being observed from a distance by someone else who cannot fully read her emotions but knows her situation in a general sense.
Structure: The poem is written in free verse without traditional rhyme, meter, or rhythm. It contains six stanzas, each of which could be considered simply a paragraph if this were written in prose. Lines usually begin with a new sentence or clause. The first five stanzas focus solely on the woman, the migrant; the final stanza reveals the narrator and that he or she is a fellow migrant.
Imagery: The strongest imagery in the poem is of the travel brochures--we can picture them laid out in their glossy promise. The poem also creates imagery of slow forward movement--shuffling--throughout the whole poem as the woman reluctantly makes plans to move. This is fulfilled in the final stanza as the narrator shuffles forward in line. When the narrator and his family "finger our own documents," readers can imagine that sensation.
Symbols: The travel brochures are a symbol that the life the migrant faces will be disappointing. The fact that they are described as "gaudy, competitive, plentiful" makes us distrust them. The queue the migrant and the narrator are in represents the slow, unpleasant, and reluctant process of leaving one's home unwillingly. The gate marked "For Embarking Passengers Only" is a symbol of finality; the woman will not be returning to the place that all her life she has considered her home.
In this poem A. L. Hendriks uses formal language, a detached but sympathetic tone, free verse structure, symbols, and imagery to reinforce the strong themes of loneliness and leaving home.

The Migrant
She could not remember anything about the voyage,
Her country of origin, or if someone had paid for the passage:
Of such she had no recollection.
She was sure only that she had travelled;
Without doubt had been made welcome.
For a while she believed she was home,
Rooted and securely settled,
Until it was broken to her
That in fact she was merely in transit
Bound for some other destination,
Committed to continue elsewhere.
This slow realization sharpened,
She formed plans to postpone her departure
Not observing her movement en route to the exit.
When she did, it was piteous how, saddened,
She went appreciably closer towards it.
Eventually facing the inescapable
She began reading travel brochures,
(Gaudy, competitive, plentiful)
Spent time considering the onward journey,
Studied a new language,
Stuffed her bosom with strange currency,
Nevertheless dreading the boarding announcements.
We watch her go through
The gate for ‘Embarking Passengers Only’,
Fearful and unutterably lonely,
Finger our own documents,
Shuffle forward in the queue.

What is Tone in a Poem?
Tone is the poet’s attitude toward the subject they are writing about.It tells us how the speaker feels—angry, hopeful, sad, mocking, grateful, fearful, exhausted, etc.
Look for:
Adjectives (harsh, weary, tired, hopeful, bright, bleak)
Verbs (drift, march, flee, cling)
Figurative language (metaphors, personification, irony)
Descriptive details (weather, environment, movement, dialogue)
How to identify tone
Ask:
What feelings or attitude does the speaker show?
Are they judging the situation or simply describing it?
Do they sound emotional or detached?
Tone often shifts, so noticing changes is important.
What is Mood in a Poem?
Mood is the feeling the poem creates in the reader.It is about the emotional atmosphere — calm, tense, gloomy, nostalgic, peaceful, uncertain, joyful.
Look for:
Imagery (visual pictures, sounds, smells)
Setting (place, time, weather)
Repetition (creates anxiety, rhythm, or calm)
Diction (word choice that feels heavy, light, dark, bright)
How to identify mood
Ask:
What emotions do I feel while reading?
What images or words create these emotions?
Mood is created in the reader, but guided by the language the poet chooses.
Key difference
Tone = Poet/Speaker’s attitude
Mood = Reader’s emotional response
Tone comes from the writer.Mood happens in the reader.

Their being placed in the wire basket serves as a metaphor for the meagre dwelling and its dwellers, reinstating the fragility of hope throbbing within. Despite all odds, the dwelling is up and standing. Just like the eggs held together by a wire basket. The poet sees it as an expression of faith.
Background of the poet and her work
Imtiaz Dharker (1954 - ) has explained that the poem describes the slums of Mumbai, where people migrate from all over India in the hope of a better life. The slum areas are living spaces created out of all kinds of found materials: corrugated sheets, wooden beams and tarpaulin. In this poem she celebrates the existence of these living spaces as a miracle. It may seem like an act of faith to live in one of these rough structures - a daring attempt to live in such a place.
Dharker was born in Lahore, Punjab, Pakistan. She grew up in Glasgow where her family moved when she was less than one year old. The main themes of Dharker's poetry include home, freedom, journeys, geographical and cultural displacement, communal conflict and gender politics. Dharker is seen as one of Britain's most inspirational contemporary poets. She is also a documentary filmmaker, having lived and worked in Mumbai, and divides her time between India and the UK.
Which are the key themes arising out of the poem?
Fragility of the human condition: The building structure and the eggs as well as the wire basket, as described by the poet, are all fragile. They symbolise the fragility of the human condition, especially when not backed by power and privilege which gives access to resources.
The evidence for this is found in lines: ‘Beams crookedly balance on supports thrust off the vertical’; ‘the whole structure leans dangerously towards the miraculous’; and, ‘Into this rough frame, someone has squeezed a living space and even dared to place these eggs in a wire basket, fragile curves of white hung out over the dark edge’.
Hope and Faith: The lines that describe the fragility also extend towards portrayals of faith as the dwellers rely on their sense of hope for the morrow by creating a living space for themselves against all odds. Despite their lack of access to resources, they have mustered whatever they could to build their dwelling, however unstable it may seem. They stay on in these spaces, living out their mundane routines. The evidence for this is in the following lines: ‘the whole structure leans dangerously towards the miraculous’; and, ‘Into this rough frame, someone has squeezed a living space and even dared to place these eggs in a wire basket, fragile curves of white hung out over the dark edge of a slanted universe, gathering the light into themselves, as if they were the bright, thin walls of faith’.
Line by line analysis of the structure of the poem and its poetic devices
Structure: The poem is structured as a free verse, with no rhyming scheme or words. The structure itself provides an analogy for the living space the poem describes: un-ordered, various objects put together randomly but intentionally by the dwellers to maximize their resources. The poet uses brief lines with enjambments all through, creating an unstructured verse.
Tone: The poet’s attitude towards the subject – the dwelling and its dwellers is that of silent admiration amidst a sense of foreboding over the fragility of the living space. The poem begins by drawing the reader’s attention to the fragility of the meagre dwellings, put together in a ramshackle manner. It shifts towards celebration of its inhabitants ability to be creative, courageous, and hopeful by using the eggs in the wire basket, described as ‘gathering light into themselves’ to symbolize life in the midst of the forces of uncertainty.
Mood: The poet uses juxtaposition of ideas such as creativity and resourcefulness in the face of poverty, resilience in the face of uncertainty, hope and faith in the face of lack of power and privilege to effectively create an atmosphere of appreciation and admiration for the inhabitants of the living space described. As a reader, we are drawn to appreciate the creativity required to construct a living space with meagre resources, through the lines that indicate there are ‘no straight lines’; ‘nails clutching as open seams’ while the ‘whole structure leans dangerously towards the miraculous’. We also feel admiration and hope as we learn that in this space, someone has placed a wire basket filled with eggs, their white walls taking in the light that contrasts with the darkness of their surroundings overall. It signifies contentment of its dwellers against the instability of their present lives.

Impact of literary Devices in a Speech
1. Vivid Imagery:
Significance: Similes and metaphors create vivid mental images by comparing one thing to another. They use familiar or concrete objects to represent abstract ideas, making the speech more imaginative and engaging.
2. Emotional Impact:
Significance: Metaphors, especially, can evoke strong emotions. They allow speakers to convey complex emotions indirectly, connecting with the audience on a deeper, more personal level.
3. Simplifying Complex Ideas:
Significance: Both similes and metaphors simplify complex or abstract concepts by likening them to something more tangible. This aids in audience comprehension, making intricate ideas more accessible.
4. Enhanced Persuasion:
Significance: Comparing a product, idea, or argument to something positive through similes and metaphors can enhance its appeal. It makes persuasive speeches more convincing by creating positive associations in the listeners' minds.
5. Memorability:
Significance: The imagery created by similes and metaphors often makes the speech more memorable. Listeners are more likely to remember a speech that paints a vivid picture in their minds.
6. Cultural and Historical Context:
Significance: Metaphors and similes often draw on cultural or historical references. This connection to shared experiences enhances understanding and resonance among diverse audiences.
7. Rhetorical Flourish:
Significance: Similes and metaphors add a touch of elegance and style to the speech. They are a form of rhetorical flourish that captures attention and enhances the overall aesthetic appeal of the language used.
Encouraging Reflection:
Significance: Rhetorical questions prompt listeners to reflect on the topic. When used effectively, they encourage the audience to think critically, analyze the speech content, and form their own conclusions.
Creating Dramatic Effect:
Significance: Rhetorical questions can create a sense of drama and anticipation in a speech. By posing a question without immediately providing an answer, speakers build tension, keeping the audience engaged until the answer is revealed or implied.
8. Capturing Nuances:
Significance: Certain feelings or nuances are challenging to express directly. Metaphors, by their symbolic nature, allow speakers to convey subtle emotions and abstract concepts more effectively.
9. Highlighting Similarities and Contrasts:
Significance: Similes and metaphors can emphasize similarities between two dissimilar things (simile) or highlight the shared characteristics of two unlike objects (metaphor). This aids in making a point or drawing comparisons.
10. Encouraging Reflection:
Significance: Poetic language, including similes and metaphors, encourages listeners to reflect on the speech's meaning. The figurative language often prompts deeper thought and contemplation.

Writing a speech
Overview
The purpose of a speech is often to inform or persuade an audience. Speeches are usually written to be spoken directly
to an audience and can be used to entertain, influencing the listeners that the viewpoint of the speaker is correct. Speeches
can also be used to encourage the audience to take action or to change their behaviour in some way; for example, to join
a particular school club or society, or to recycle more. The ways you use language and vocabulary when writing the words
of  a  speech  will  depend  on  the  audience  and  the  purpose  you  are  writing  for;  for  example,  in  a  speech  to  a  group  of
teachers and parents giving your views on a recent proposal, formal language is most appropriate.
Tips for writing a speech
Language – think about:
•
the audience that the speech is for – are you giving your speech to a group of people you know, or
do not know, or a mixture of both? If you know your audience well, you may be able to relax a little, but
a speech is still a formal kind of talk and would usually not include slang.
•  whether your audience are likely to disagree with what you say – you will need to consider any
possible objections and deal with them. Use language carefully to make objections seem less
significant; for example, using phrases like ‘A few people may still think, however’.
•
the reason you are giving this speech and how you feel about this topic – try to imagine the
words of your speech as you would speak them out loud. Your tone of voice must match your
message, so choose words that appeal to the emotions of your listeners. Focus on what you want
your audience to know and feel by the end of your speech.
•  how to engage your listeners – for example, you might use inclusive words or phrases like ‘we’, ‘all
of us’ and ‘our’ to make your listeners feel that you are all on the same side.
Organisation:
•  Plan where you want to finish your speech and how you will get there before you start writing.
The structure of a speech is often in three parts. For example:
1.  an opening that grabs your audience’s attention and makes the overall topic of your
speech clear – for example, pose a question to the audience where you can predict the answer.
2.  a well-structured, supported and developed argument – for example, to support your
argument you might use real life examples or anecdotes.
3.  a powerful conclusion – for example, group your final words or ideas in threes to help make
them memorable or end with a thought- provoking question or image and thank your audience for
listening.
•  Organise your ideas into paragraphs as appropriate – this will help you to develop and support
your points convincingly, to build your argument and/or offer a full explanation of a particular point of
view.
•  Show the connections between ideas in sentences and paragraphs – where a new point or idea
follows on from what you have already said you might use linking words or phrases such as,‘in
addition’, ‘likewise’ or ‘similarly’.

Who Is Imtiaz Dharker? — Biography & Literary Profile
Imtiaz Dharker is a Pakistani-born British poet, artist, and documentary filmmaker. She was born on 31 January 1954 in Lahore, Pakistan, and grew up in Glasgow, Scotland after her family moved there when she was an infant.
Over her long and distinguished career, Dharker has become one of the most respected contemporary poets writing in English. Her work has received major honours — including the Queen’s Gold Medal for Poetry (2014), election as a Fellow of the Royal Society of Literature (2011), and she has served as Chancellor of Newcastle University since 2020.
The poem “Blessing” appears in the collection Postcards from God, which was published in the 1990s (mid-1990s) and later republished with other early work. Different sources place the poem’s origin within Dharker’s early published work in the late 1980s or 1990s (e.g., it was associated with her earlier Purdah (1989) and later included in Postcards from God).
Dharker’s poems, including “Blessing,” are widely anthologised and studied, and the poem is included in British GCSE and IGCSE anthologies used in English education in the UK and internationally.
Across her work, Dharker explores several recurring themes:
Home and displacement
Freedom and journeys
Geographical and cultural identity
Social inequality and communal life
Gender politics and human rights
These themes arise out of her personal life experiences — growing up between cultures (Pakistan and Scotland), living in Mumbai (Bombay) for many years, and observing urban life and poverty firsthand. Her poetry often reflects the cross-cultural tensions of global modern life as well as very real historical and social conditions that people experience — such as poverty, scarcity, migration, and survival.
The poem’s relevance to the unit – Historical fiction
“Blessing” is set in an urban slum in Mumbai, where basic infrastructure (such as water supply) is unreliable. This reflects real historical and social conditions of urban life in many developing cities. The poem’s depiction of scarcity and the way communities respond to it gives students insight into:
how people lived,
how environmental conditions shaped daily life, and
how historical realities affect human experience.
This is similar to historical fiction, where imagined characters live through real conditions. Dharker’s poem imagines voices reacting to scarcity and survival. The experience of water shortage, the sense of migration and temporary accommodation in areas like Dharavi (Asia’s biggest slum), and the urgency of communal response all connect to larger histories of urbanisation, migration, and inequality. As a student, you can make meaningful text–world connections between the poem and broader historical forces — just as in historical fiction.
The poem uses personal perspective to illuminate shared experience
Like many classic historical fiction texts, Dharker’s work gives a personal, human voice to conditions that are real and historically situated. Instead of telling a dry fact, the poem’s speaker allows readers to feel the lived experience of these conditions, much as historical fiction does.
The poem’s imagery — such as an old municipal pipe bursting, children playing in the sudden water, and adults scrambling to collect it — points to everyday realities shaped by economic and social history. Though Dharker does not name a specific year in the poem, the urban landscape she evokes — inconsistent water access, slum-like settlement patterns — is rooted in documented social realities, especially around the city of Mumbai where she has lived and worked.
In this way, “Blessing” not only describes a moment but also situates it within a historically informed lived experience — much like the characters in a historical fiction narrative whose lives are shaped by real social forces.

FAHRENHEIT 451 by Ray Bradbury
I. Outline: Fahrenheit 451 is Ray Bradbury’s 1953 dystopian novel set in a future society where books are banned, firemen burn them, and citizens are numbed by screens, noise, and superficial pleasure. The story follows Guy Montag, a fireman who begins questioning his society, rebels, and ultimately seeks meaning in a community that preserves books through memory. At its core, the novel explores themes like censorship, conformity, the death of critical thinking, and the power of literature to preserve humanity.
II. Main Characters & Their Relationships
1. Guy Montag: The protagonist - a loyal fireman at the start, later a rebel intellectual-in-the-making. His relationship to others marks his transformation. He is the emerging hero in this dystopian story.
2. Mildred Montag: Montag’s emotionally numb, screen-addicted wife. Their relationship is empty and distant. She symbolizes society’s collective apathy.
3. Clarisse McClellan: Montag’s 17-year-old neighbour, curious, reflective, and labelled “antisocial” by the state. Her brief conversations awaken Montag’s mind. She serves as his catalyst for change.
4. Captain Beatty: Montag’s fire chief, intelligent but cynical. He understands books well but believes they are dangerous. Both mentor and antagonist. He is the anti-hero in this novel.
5. Faber: An old English professor who secretly opposes censorship. He becomes Montag’s mentor and guide in resisting the regime.
6. Granger: Leader of a group of intellectual exiles living outside the city. He welcomes Montag into the “book people”.
III. Detailed Plot Summary (make a story mountain)
1. Exposition: Montag begins as a content fireman who “enjoys burning things” (short quote). He meets Clarisse, who asks the piercing question: “Are you happy?” This moment plants doubt in him and triggers his critical thinking.
2. Rising Action: These are key developments in Montag’s awakening:
Checkpoint 1 — The old woman burns herself: During a raid, an old woman refuses to leave her books and dies with them. Short quote: “You can’t ever have my books.” This horrifies Montag and shakes his faith in firemen.
Checkpoint 2 — Clarisse disappears: Clarisse mysteriously disappears (later revealed dead). Montag feels her absence deeply — emotional upheaval intensifies.
Checkpoint 3 — Montag steals a book: He secretly begins reading. Mildred refuses to engage with him. State pressure increases.
Checkpoint 4 — Visit to Faber: Montag seeks out Faber, who explains why books matter and gives him a communication device for guidance.
Checkpoint 5 — Confrontation at home: Montag is forced to burn his own house after Mildred reports him. Short quote: “Wasn’t it I, Montag, who turned in the alarm?” (Beatty)
3. Climax: Montag kills Captain Beatty with a flamethrower after Beatty discovers Faber’s device and taunts him. Quote: “We’ll trace this and burn your friend.” This moment marks Montag’s irreversible break from the regime.
4. Falling Action: Montag becomes a fugitive. The Mechanical Hound hunts him. He escapes the city and meets Granger’s group, who preserve books by memorising them. A fake Montag is executed on television to maintain public order. Society continues its illusion.
5. Resolution: The city is destroyed by bombs. Quote: “The city rolled over and fell.” Montag and the book people walk toward the ruins to rebuild civilisation, guided by literature and memory.
IV. Explanation of the Climax & Meaning of the Book
Climax Meaning (Montag kills Beatty)
The climax represents:
Montag’s final rejection of state authority
The triumph of individual conscience over tyranny
The collapse of his former identity as a fireman
Beatty’s death symbolizes the breaking of censorship’s grip over Montag’s mind.
Meaning of the Book as a Whole
Bradbury warns of:
The spiritual and intellectual death caused by censorship
The dangers of passive entertainment and distraction
The necessity of critical thinking, empathy, and literature
The responsibility of individuals to resist unjust systems
Ultimately, the book argues that books preserve the depth and complexity of human experience, and society collapses when it silences thought.

Simplified Explainer: Analysing Print Advertisements
1. The Rhetorical Triangle: The rhetorical triangle helps us understand how advertisements persuade us. It has three parts:
a. Ethos – Trust: Advertisers try to show that they are trustworthy, expert, or reliable. They want you to think: “I can believe this brand!”
b. Pathos – Feelings: They use techniques that make you feel something – excitement, fear, happiness, hope, or even sadness. Feelings push us to buy.
c. Logos – Facts & Logic: Advertisers use facts, numbers, or reasons to show that their product is helpful or better.
2. Four Common Advertising Techniques: These are tricks or methods that print ads use to catch attention and convince people.
a. Fear Appeal: The ad makes you feel scared or worried about something so that you buy the product to stay safe.
Example: "If you don’t use this toothpaste, your teeth may decay!"
b. Tall Claims: The ad makes big, exaggerated promises that are usually impossible to prove.
Example: "This cream will make you look younger in 24 hours!"
c. Bandwagon: The ad suggests that everyone is using this product, so you should too.
Example: "Join millions who have switched to EcoWater bottles!"
d. Testimonial: The ad uses a famous person or a real user who says the product is good.
Example: A cricketer promoting an energy drink.
Quick Tips for Analysis
When you see a print advertisement, ask:
What feeling is this ad trying to create? (Pathos)
Why should I trust this brand? (Ethos)
What facts or reasons are they giving me? (Logos)
Which technique is used: fear appeal, tall claim, bandwagon, or testimonial?

Elements of a Print Advertisement (Simple Explainer)
1. Advertiser: This is the company or brand that is selling the product or service.
Example: McDonald’s, Nike, Apple.
2. Slogan: A slogan is a short, catchy line used for a specific product or campaign. It can change from one advertisement to another.
Example: “Limited-time spicy deal!”
3. Tagline: A tagline is a permanent line that represents the brand’s identity.
It usually stays the same for many years.
Example: Nike’s “Just Do It.”
Key Difference: Slogan vs Tagline
Slogan = campaign-specific, may change often.
Tagline = brand-specific, stays the same for a long time.
Think of it like this:
Slogan = short-term message
Tagline = long-term message
4. Imagery: These are the pictures, photos, colours, or drawings in the ad.
Imagery grabs attention and creates feelings (hunger, excitement, fun).
5. Body Copy: This is the main text or information in the ad.
It explains the product, gives details, or supports the message.
6. Logo: The logo is the symbol or design that represents the brand.
It helps people recognize the company instantly.
Example: McDonald’s golden arches (the yellow “M”).
7. Call-to-Action (CTA): A call-to-action tells the reader what to do next.
It encourages immediate action.
Examples: “Buy now,” “Try today,” “Order online.”
