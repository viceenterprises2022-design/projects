1. Balanced Diet
🔹 Definition
A balanced diet is a diet that provides all the essential nutrients — carbohydrates, proteins,
fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre, and water — in the right amounts to maintain health, support
growth, and provide energy.
🔹 Importance of a Balanced Diet
•  Energy: Carbohydrates and fats fuel daily activities.
•  Growth & Repair: Proteins build and repair body tissues.
•  Immunity: Vitamins and minerals strengthen immune defense.
•  Healthy body functions: Fibre aids digestion; water maintains hydration and
circulation.
•  Prevents diseases like malnutrition, obesity, anaemia, scurvy, rickets, and lifestyle
diseases such as diabetes and heart disease.

3. Interpreting Food Pyramids / Plates
•  Food Pyramid / Plate = A guide showing how much of each food group to eat daily.
•  Key features:
o  Base: Carbohydrates (whole grains).
o  Next: Fruits & Vegetables (vitamins, minerals, fibre).
o  Next: Proteins (meat, fish, eggs, pulses) and Dairy.
o  Top: Fats, oils, and sweets (eat sparingly).
•  Plate method (WHO / MyPlate): Half plate = fruits + vegetables, 1/4 plate = grains,
1/4 plate = proteins + side serving of dairy.
4. Dietary Requirements
A. Based on Age
•  Infancy: High fat and calcium (milk-based diet) for rapid growth.
•  Childhood: Balanced intake of all nutrients for growth and activity.
•  Adolescence: More protein, calcium, iron, and calories (due to puberty and growth
spurts).
•  Adulthood: Balanced diet with moderate calories; avoid excess fats and sugar.
•  Elderly: Fewer calories but more vitamins (especially Vitamin D, calcium) for bone
and immune health.
B. Based on Gender
•  Males: Generally need more calories and proteins due to higher muscle mass.
•  Females: Require extra iron (to replace menstrual blood loss), and during pregnancy,
higher folic acid, calcium, and proteins.
C. Based on Activity
•  Sedentary lifestyle: Lower calorie needs; risk of obesity if overeating.
•  Active lifestyle / athletes: High carbohydrates for energy, high protein for muscle
recovery, more water and electrolytes.
🔹 Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)
•  The minimum energy the body requires to function at rest (for breathing, heart
beating, maintaining body temperature).
•  BMR depends on age, gender, body size, and activity level.

6. Factors Influencing Food Choices
•  Cultural & Religious Beliefs: Hindus avoid beef, Muslims avoid pork, vegetarians
avoid meat.
•  Personal Preferences: Likes, dislikes, allergies.
•  Nutritional Knowledge: Awareness of health and diet impacts.
•  Socioeconomic Status: Income affects food affordability.
•  Availability & Accessibility: Seasonal and regional foods.
•  Marketing & Advertising: Media influence, celebrity endorsements, colorful
packaging.
7. Reading and Analyzing Nutrition Labels
What to look for on food packaging:
•  Serving size: Understand how much the values apply to.
•  Calorie content: Energy per serving.
•  Macronutrient breakdown: Carbs, proteins, fats (total and saturated).
•  Micronutrients: Vitamins and minerals like calcium, iron.
•  Ingredients list: Usually arranged from highest to lowest quantity.
•  Added sugars and sodium: Check for health risks.
8. Marketing Strategies & USP (Unique Selling Proposition)
🔹 Marketing Strategies in Food Advertising
•  Attractive packaging (bright colors, images).
•  Use of slogans and jingles.
•  Celebrity endorsements.
•  Health claims (“low-fat,” “sugar-free”).
•  Free toys with children’s meals.
🔹 Unique Selling Proposition (USP)
•  The special feature that makes a product different from competitors.
•  Example:
o  A cereal brand claiming “high in fibre” (health USP).
o  A chocolate claiming “melts in your mouth, not in your hand” (quality USP).
🔹 Critical Analysis of Advertisements
•  Question if claims are scientifically true or just marketing tricks.
•  Example: A soft drink ad may show energy and fun, but in reality it is high in sugar.
•  Students should learn to separate facts from persuasion.
