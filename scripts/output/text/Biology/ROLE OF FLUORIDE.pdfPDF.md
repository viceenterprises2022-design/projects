1) Fast facts
•  Fluoride (F⁻) is a naturally occurring ion of the element fluorine.
•  Target tissue: Tooth enamel (mainly hydroxyapatite: Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂).
•  Core actions (3):
o  Reduces demineralization of enamel in acid.
o  Enhances remineralization—helps rebuild early enamel damage.
o  Inhibits bacterial acid production in dental plaque.
•  Key chemistry: Fluoride helps form fluorapatite (Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆F₂), which is more acid-
resistant than hydroxyapatite.
•  Critical pH: ~5.5 for hydroxyapatite vs ~4.5 for fluorapatite (approximate).
•  Main sources: Fluoridated toothpaste, varnish/gel (by professionals), mouth rinses,
and sometimes community water (where implemented).
•  Benefits: Fewer cavities, slowed progression of early lesions, lower treatment needs.
•  Risks (dose-related): Dental fluorosis if excess fluoride is ingested during enamel
formation; rare acute upset from swallowing large amounts of toothpaste.
•  Smart use: Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste; spit, don’t vigorously rinse;
supervise younger children’s toothpaste amount.
2) How tooth decay happens
•  Plaque biofilm contains bacteria (e.g., Streptococcus mutans) that ferment dietary
sugars.
•  Acids produced lower pH at the tooth surface → demineralization (minerals leave
enamel).
•  Between meals, saliva (with calcium/phosphate) helps remineralize enamel.
•  Decay = imbalance: too much demineralization vs remineralization over time.
3) What is fluoride and how it works
A. Chemistry & enamel
•  Fluoride can integrate near the enamel surface to form fluorapatite, which dissolves
less readily in acid than hydroxyapatite.
•  This lowers enamel’s solubility and raises acid resistance, effectively shifting the
balance toward enamel survival.
B. Remineralization enhancer
•
In the presence of saliva (source of Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻), low concentrations of fluoride:
o  Accelerate crystal regrowth on early “white-spot” lesions.
o  Encourage formation of larger, less porous crystals that are harder to
dissolve next time.
C. Antibacterial effects (plaque biofilm)
•  Fluoride inhibits bacterial enzymes (e.g., enolase in glycolysis), reducing acid
production after sugar intake.
•  As HF in acidic plaque, it can enter bacteria and disrupt metabolism, slowing acid
formation.
Exam tip: State the three mechanisms clearly—marker-friendly and often asked.

5) Benefits and potential risks
Benefits
•  Prevents new cavities and slows/arrests early lesions.
•  Protects all tooth surfaces, including early pits and fissures.
•  Population benefit where water is fluoridated—everyone receives baseline
protection, including those with less access to dental care.
•  Cost-effective at scale (important for public health).
Risks (mainly dose- and age-dependent)
•  Dental fluorosis: Cosmetic changes (white flecks to brown stains) if excess fluoride
is ingested while teeth are developing (roughly up to ~8 years old for most permanent
teeth).
•  Acute ingestion: Swallowing large amounts of high-concentration products can cause
stomach upset; true toxicity is rare and requires medical attention.
•  High natural fluoride in groundwater can cause skeletal fluorosis in adults over
many years—managed by defluoridation programs.
Balance statement for exams: “At recommended levels, benefits substantially outweigh risks;
risk management focuses on appropriate dosing and supervision.”
6) Ethics, society, and the environment
•  Public health vs individual autonomy: Water fluoridation aims for equitable
prevention, but some view it as “mass medication.”
•  Consent: Individual consent is not practical for municipal water; ethical analysis
weighs collective benefit vs autonomy, similar to fortifying salt with iodine or flour
with folate.
•  Equity: Fluoridation particularly helps high-risk groups with limited dental access—
supports health equity.
•  Environmental aspects: Fluoride occurs naturally; programs monitor source,
dosing, and wastewater to keep levels within safe limits.
•  Governance: Decisions typically involve risk–benefit reviews, expert guidance,
and community consultation.

8) Common misconceptions (and correct statements)
•  “Fluoride completely heals cavities.”
✗ Incorrect. It can remineralize early lesions (white spots) but cannot restore
deep cavities—those need restorative care.
•  “More fluoride is always better.”
✗ Incorrect. Right dose, right route is key; excess can cause fluorosis.
•  “Fluoridated water removes the need for brushing.”
✗ Incorrect. Mechanical plaque removal + fluoride is essential.
