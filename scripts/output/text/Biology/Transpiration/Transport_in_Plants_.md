Transport in Plants
Xylem, Phloem, Water Uptake,
Transpiration, and Translocation
Xylem and Phloem (Vascular Bundles)
• Xylem: Transports water and minerals from roots to
leaves
• Phloem: Transports sugars (mainly sucrose) and
amino acids
• Xylem is made of dead, lignified cells for support
• Phloem consists of living cells with sieve tubes and
companion cells
• Both form vascular bundles for efficient transport
Adaptations of xylem vessels
Water Uptake and Structure of Root
Hair
• Root hairs increase surface area for water absorption
• Water enters by osmosis from soil (higher water
potential) into root cells
• Thin cell walls and close contact with soil particles
aid absorption
• Minerals absorbed by active transport

Potometer
Experiment
to Measure
Transpiration
Rate
Potometer measures water uptake
as an estimate of transpiration
Setup includes capillary tube,
water reservoir, and plant shoot
Movement of air bubble indicates
water uptake
Controlled variables: light,
temperature, humidity, wind
Potometer
How a potometer is used (step-by-step)
• Cut a leafy plant shoot underwater to prevent air
bubbles entering the xylem.
• Fix the shoot into the potometer apparatus securely
so it is airtight.
• Fill the apparatus completely with water.
• Introduce a small air bubble into the capillary tube.
• Allow the plant to transpire.
• Measure the distance moved by the air bubble over
time using a ruler.
•
Faster bubble movement = higher rate of water
uptake = higher transpiration rate.
Conditions
that Affect
Transpiration
Rate
Light intensity – opens stomata,
increases transpiration
Temperature – increases evaporation
rate
Humidity – high humidity lowers
transpiration
Wind – removes water vapor,
increases transpiration
Soil water availability – low water
limits transpiration
Wilting
Occurs when water loss exceeds
uptake
Cells lose turgor pressure,
causing leaves to droop
Temporary wilting: recovers at
night when transpiration is lower
Permanent wilting: plant cannot
recover, may die
Translocation
and Source-
Sink Concept
Translocation: Movement of
sugars and amino acids in phloem
Source: Site of production (e.g.,
leaves)
Sink: Site of use or storage (e.g.,
roots, fruits)
Transport is bidirectional, unlike
xylem
