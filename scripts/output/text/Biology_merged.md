#49 Diet
The food an animal eat everyday is called diet. Most animals need 7 types of
nutrients in their diet: carbohydrates, proteins, fats +  water, fibre,
vitamins, minerals.
The amount of energy needed is provided mainly by our carbohydrate and
fat intake. Your dietary requirements depend on your age, sex and activity.
- Age: The energy demand increases until we stop growing. While children
are growing they need more protein per kilogram of body weight than adults
do.
- Sex: Generally, males use up more energy than females.
- Pregnant women need extra nutrients for the development of the fetus.
A. A balanced diet is a diet that contains all the main nutrients in the
correct amounts and proportions to maintain good health.
B. Malnutrition is the result of not eating a balanced diet. There may be:
  wrong amount of food: too little or too much


incorrect proportion of main nutrients
lacking in one or more key nutrients
Effects of malnutrition
1. Obesity - Too much food (carbohydrate, fat or protein)
2. Coronary heart disease
  Too much saturated/animal fat in the diet results in high cholesterol
levels.
  Cholesterol can stick to the walls of arteries, gradually blocking them.
If coronary arteries become blocked, the results can be angina and

coronary heart disease.
3. Starvation
  Too little food can result in starvation.
  Extreme slimming diets, such as those that avoid carbohydrate foods,
can result in the disease anorexia nervosa.
Starvation
4. Childhood protein-energy malnutrition (Kwashiakor)
Wrong proportion of nutrients e.g. too much carbohydrates (starchy foods)
and a lack of protein can lead to Kwashiakor in young children.
Kwashiakor characterized by edema,
anorexia, ulcerating dermatoses.
5. Vitamin, mineral and fiber deficiency diseases - Lacking key
nutrients.

#54 Adding fluoride to public water supplies
Fluoride helps prevent destruction of the tooth surface caused
by acids produced by bacteria. It forms a reservoir on the teeth from
which fluoride is released during attack. It reduces the loss of
minerals from the tooth and promotes repair of early tooth decay.
Growing children can absorb fluoride in their diet (from toothpaste of
fluoridated water). It becomes part of the enamel of their developing teeth,
and the enamel; is then more resistant to tooth decay.
Arguments for and against the addition of fluoride to public water
supplies
For:
  Tooth decay in the local population of children decreases.
  There is no need to buy fluoridated toothpaste.
Against:

It is form of mass medication – people have no choice  about whether
or not they want the treatment.
  Fluoride is a benefit only to growing children – adults do not benefit.

If people take proper care of their teeth, fluoridation is unnecessary.
  Fluoride may have side effects, such as an increase in risk of bone
cancer (but this is unlikely).
Proper care of teeth
  Avoid sugary food, especially between meals, so bacteria cannot make
acid and clean teeth regularly to remove plaque.
  Use dental floss or a toothpick to remove pieces of food and plaque
trapped between them.
  Use fluoride toothpaste (or drink fluoridated water) – fluoride hardens
tooth enamel.
  Visit a dentist regularly to make sure and tooth decay is reacted early
and any stubborn plaque (called calculus) is removed.
Common misconceptions
There is a big difference between fluoride and fluorine. Fluorine is a very
toxic gas, while fluoride is a mineral that helps to strengthen teeth. Make
sure do not use the term fluorine in an exam answer about teeth.
#55 Mechanical and chemical digestion
Food that we ingest is mainly made up of large, insoluble molecules that
can not be absorbed through the gut wall. It needs to be changed
into small, soluble molecules.
1. Mechanical digestion is the physical process of preparing the food for
chemical digestion.

It involves chewing (in the mouth), mixing, churning (in the
stomach and intestine) and segmentation (in the intestine).
  Large pieces of food are breaking down into smaller pieces à increases
the surface area of the food.
  Bile physically digests fats by emulsifying them – turning them into
small droplets with a large surface area.
Chewing
Mechanical digestion, performed bye the teeth à pieces of food are mixed
with saliva and become smaller à easier to swallow and have a larger surface
area.
Peristalsis
  The walls of the alimentary canal have an inner, circular
muscle fibre coat and an outer, longitudinal muscle fibre coat.
  As the ball of food (bolus) formed in the mouth enters the pharynx, a
reflex action is initiated.
  This produces slow, wave-like contractions in the walls of the
esophagus and later along the whole length of the tract (peristalsis).
  Peristaltic waves involve the contraction of the circular
muscle fibres behind the bolus (A) and their relaxation in front of
the bolus.
  Longitudinal muscles provide the wave-like action. The two functions
together push the ball down the tract (B).
Misconceptions: Chewing food does not involve breaking down large
molecules into small molecules; it only breaks down large pieces into smaller
pieces, giving a larger surface area for enzymes to work on.
Video Peristalsis
2. Chemical digestion

Involves breaking down large, insoluble molecules into small, soluble
ones.
  Enzymes speed up the process. They work efficiently at body
temperature (370C) and at suitable pH.
  The main places where chemical digestion happens are the mouth,
stomach and small intestine.
# 56 Absorption – function of small intestine and significance of villi
Absorption is the movement of digested food molecules through the
wall of the intestine into the blood or lymph.
Digestion is completed in the small intestine. By now, most carbohydrates
have been broken down to simple sugar, proteins to amino acids, and fats
to fatty acids and glycerol. These molecules are small enough to pass
through the wall of the small intestine and into the blood. This is
called absorption. The  small  intestine is especially adapted to allow
absorption to take place very efficiently.
It has a very rich blood supply. Digested food molecules are small enough to
pass through the wall of the intestine into the bloodstream. Water, mineral
salts and vitamins are also absorbed in the small instestine.
The small intestine absorbs 5-10 dm3 of water each day. How ever, the
colon absorbs much less water and salt than the small intestine, generally
around 0,3–0,5 dm3 per day.
Significance of Villii
Villi are finger like projections that increase the surface area for absorption.
If a section of small intestine was turned inside out, its surface would be kike
a carpet. Inside each villus are:
- Blood capillaries: absorb amino acids and glucose.
- Lacteals: absorb fatty acids and glycerol.
Food molecules are absorbed:
- mainly by diffusion.
- or by active transport.
Epithelial cells contain mitochondria to provide energy for absorption against
the concentration gradient.
Role of the hepatic portal vein
The hepatic portal vein transports absorbed food from the small intestine to
the liver. After a meal, the blood in this vein contains very high
concentrations of glucose and amino acids, as well as vitamins and minerals.
The liver reduces levels backs to normal.

#57 Assimilation and role of the liver
Assimilation is the movement of digested food  molecules into
the  cells  of the  body  where they  are  used, becoming part  of the  cells.
Role of liver in the metabolism of glucose and amino acids
  Excess glucose in the blood arriving at the liver is converted
into glycogen (animal starch) for storage, or broken down through
respiration, producing energy for other purposes.
  Amino acids cannot be stored in our body, so any that is excess has to
be dealt with in the liver.
- Some amino acids are transaminated to produce a different amino
acid.
- The rest are deaminated to produce ammonia (NH3) and a keto
acid.
+ NH3 is converted into urea, which is transported to the kidneys and
excreted.
+ The keto acid is used primarily as energy for liver cells
So Deamination is the removing of nitrogen-containing part of
amino acids to form urea and using of the remainder of amino acid to
provide energy to the liver cells.
Role of liver in the breaking down of alcohol  and  other  toxins
  Breaking down any toxins absorbed from the alimentary canal,
including drugs such as alcohol. Cells in the liver are able to
convert many toxins to harmless substances that can be
transported in the blood and excreted from the body.
Role of fat as  an  energy storage substance
  Fatty acids and glycerol pass into the lymphatic system and then the
bloodstream. Once in the blood nutrients are carried to all cells of the
body. Some are oxidised to produce energy and others are used to
repair the cell, build new cells.
  Fat is a good storage compound – it releases twice as much energy as
carbohydrates when respired, and act as insulation in the skin. Some
nerve cells form a myelin sheath from fat, to prevent electrical
impulses from leaking out.
#58 Summary of animal nutrition
A balanced diet contains suitable proportions of each group of nutrients –
carbohydrates, fats, proteins, minerals, vitamins, water and fibre – and the
correct amount of energy.
  Eating food containing more energy than you can use up causes
weight increase, which can lead to obesity. Children who do not get
enough food may suffer from energy protein malnutrition, in which
they do not grow properly and have little energy.
  Digestion is the breakdown of large molecules of food into small ones,
so that they can be absorbed through the wall of the alimentary canal.
  Mechanical digestion breaks down large pieces of food to small ones. It
is done by the teeth, the muscles in the wall of the alimentary canal
and bile salts. Chemical digestion breaks down large molecules to
small ones. It is done by enzymes.
  Mammals have four types of teeth – incisors, canines, premolars and
molars – each with their own functions.
  Digestion begins in the mouth, as teeth grind food into smaller pieces,
and amylase digests starch to maltose.
  Protein digestion begins in the stomach, where pepsin digests proteins
to polypeptides. Rennin is present in young mammals, and clots milk
protein. Hydrochloric acid kills bacteria and provides a low pH for the
action of pepsin.
  Pancreatic juice flows into the duodenum. It contains enzymes that
digest starch, proteins and lipids, and also sodium hydrogencarbonate
to partly neutralise the acidity of food coming from the stomach.
  Bile also flows into the duodenum. It contains bile salts, which
emulsify fats, making it easier for lipase to digest them.
  The lining of the small intestine is covered with villi, giving it a very
large surface area, which helps to speed up absorption. Cells on the
surface of the villi make enzymes, which complete the digestion of
food. The villi contain blood capillaries to absorb glucose, amino acids,
water, vitamins and minerals, and lacteals to absorb fatty acids and
glycerol.
  The absorbed nutrients are carried to the liver in the hepatic portal
vein. Some are used in the liver, some are stored, and some are sent
on in the blood to be delivered to cells all over the body.
  The colon absorbs more water from the food. In the rectum, the
undigested food is formed into faeces, which are eventually egested
through the anus.

The brown Iodine solution reacts with starch and changes it to a blue-black color. This test helps
you to find out if a food contains starch.
•Process

amylose and amylopectin.
▪ Amylose in starch is responsible for the
formation of a deep blue color in the presence
of iodine.
▪ The iodine molecule slips inside of the amylose
coil to give a special color.

Explanation
Reducing Sugars are sugars that contain aldehyde groups, that are oxidised

•They are classified as reducing sugars since they reduce the blue Cu2+(copper II
ions) to to Cu+ (copper I ions). These are precipitated in form of red Cu2O (copper
oxide), insoluble in water.
Emulsion (ethanol) test for Fats
This test is done to show the presence of lipids in a substance.
The substance is first dissolved in ethanol.
This solution is then dissolved in water. If lipids are present in the mixture, it will
precipitates and forms an emulsion
▪ Process
▪ Add the food sample to 2 cm3 of ethanol,
shake well.
▪ Allow to settle in a test tube rack for 2
minutes for food to dissolve in ethanol.
▪ Empty any clear liquid into a test tube
containing 2 cm3 of distilled H2O.
▪ A MILKY-WHITE EMULSION is a positive
result: lipid is present.
▪ If the mixture remains clear, there are no fats
present in the sample
Explanation
Lipids are insoluble in water and soluble in ethanol (an alcohol).
After lipids have been dissolved in ethanol and then added
to H2O, they will form tiny dispersed droplets in the water. This is
called an emulsion.
These droplets scatter light as it passes through the water so it
appears white and cloudy.

1) Fast facts
•  Fluoride (F⁻) is a naturally occurring ion of the element fluorine.
•  Target tissue: Tooth enamel (mainly hydroxyapatite: Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂).
•  Core actions (3):
o  Reduces demineralization of enamel in acid.
o  Enhances remineralization—helps rebuild early enamel damage.
o  Inhibits bacterial acid production in dental plaque.
•  Key chemistry: Fluoride helps form fluorapatite (Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆F₂), which is more acid-
resistant than hydroxyapatite.
•  Critical pH: ~5.5 for hydroxyapatite vs ~4.5 for fluorapatite (approximate).
•  Main sources: Fluoridated toothpaste, varnish/gel (by professionals), mouth rinses,
and sometimes community water (where implemented).
•  Benefits: Fewer cavities, slowed progression of early lesions, lower treatment needs.
•  Risks (dose-related): Dental fluorosis if excess fluoride is ingested during enamel
formation; rare acute upset from swallowing large amounts of toothpaste.
•  Smart use: Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste; spit, don’t vigorously rinse;
supervise younger children’s toothpaste amount.
2) How tooth decay happens
•  Plaque biofilm contains bacteria (e.g., Streptococcus mutans) that ferment dietary
sugars.
•  Acids produced lower pH at the tooth surface → demineralization (minerals leave
enamel).
•  Between meals, saliva (with calcium/phosphate) helps remineralize enamel.
•  Decay = imbalance: too much demineralization vs remineralization over time.
3) What is fluoride and how it works
A. Chemistry & enamel
•  Fluoride can integrate near the enamel surface to form fluorapatite, which dissolves
less readily in acid than hydroxyapatite.
•  This lowers enamel’s solubility and raises acid resistance, effectively shifting the
balance toward enamel survival.
B. Remineralization enhancer
•
In the presence of saliva (source of Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻), low concentrations of fluoride:
o  Accelerate crystal regrowth on early “white-spot” lesions.
o  Encourage formation of larger, less porous crystals that are harder to
dissolve next time.
C. Antibacterial effects (plaque biofilm)
•  Fluoride inhibits bacterial enzymes (e.g., enolase in glycolysis), reducing acid
production after sugar intake.
•  As HF in acidic plaque, it can enter bacteria and disrupt metabolism, slowing acid
formation.
Exam tip: State the three mechanisms clearly—marker-friendly and often asked.

Benefits
•  Prevents new cavities and slows/arrests early lesions.
•  Protects all tooth surfaces, including early pits and fissures.
•  Population benefit where water is fluoridated—everyone receives baseline
protection, including those with less access to dental care.
•  Cost-effective at scale (important for public health).
Risks (mainly dose- and age-dependent)

6) Ethics, society, and the environment
•  Public health vs individual autonomy: Water fluoridation aims for equitable
prevention, but some view it as “mass medication.”
•  Consent: Individual consent is not practical for municipal water; ethical analysis
weighs collective benefit vs autonomy, similar to fortifying salt with iodine or flour
with folate.
•  Equity: Fluoridation particularly helps high-risk groups with limited dental access—
supports health equity.
•  Environmental aspects: Fluoride occurs naturally; programs monitor source,
dosing, and wastewater to keep levels within safe limits.
•  Governance: Decisions typically involve risk–benefit reviews, expert guidance,
and community consultation.

8) Common misconceptions (and correct statements)
•  “Fluoride completely heals cavities.”
✗ Incorrect. It can remineralize early lesions (white spots) but cannot restore
deep cavities—those need restorative care.
•  “More fluoride is always better.”
✗ Incorrect. Right dose, right route is key; excess can cause fluorosis.
•  “Fluoridated water removes the need for brushing.”
✗ Incorrect. Mechanical plaque removal + fluoride is essential.

•  The musculoskeletal system consists of 206 bones, over 600 muscles, and
various joints working together to provide structure, protection, and
movement.
•  Bones form the rigid framework, muscles (skeletal, smooth, cardiac) generate
force by contracting, and joints—classified as synovial (movable),
cartilaginous (partially movable), or fibrous (fixed)—allow for movement
Bones (The Skeletal System)
•  Functions: Protects organs, supports body weight, and acts as leverage for
muscles.
•  Composition: Adult skeletons have 206 bones, featuring a hard exterior and
a spongy interior.
•  Key Bones: Femur (longest/strongest), skull (protects brain), ribs (protect
heart/lungs).
Muscles (The Muscular System)
•  Skeletal Muscles: Voluntary muscles attached to bones by tendons, enabling
movement. They work in pairs (flexors and extensors) to move joints.
•  Smooth Muscles: Involuntary muscles found in organs, such as the digestive
tract and blood vessels.
•  Cardiac Muscle: Involuntary muscle found only in the heart
Types of Joints (Articulations)
Joints are classified by their range of motion:
•  Synovial Joints (Freely Movable): Characterized by a synovial cavity filled
with fluid to reduce friction, allowing wide range of motion.
o  Hinge: Moves in one direction (e.g., knees, elbows).
o  Ball-and-Socket: Allows rotation and movement in many directions
(e.g., shoulder, hip).
o  Pivot: Rotates in place (e.g., neck/head joint).
o  Saddle: Curved bones fit together, moving in multiple directions
without rotation (e.g., base of thumb).
o  Condyloid/Planar: Sliding or gliding movements (e.g., wrist, between
vertebrae).
•  Cartilaginous Joints (Partially Movable): Connected by cartilage, allowing
limited movement, such as in the spine.
•  Fibrous Joints (Immovable): Connected by dense collagen tissue, providing
stability with no movement, such as the sutures in the skull.
•  Supporting Structures
•  Cartilage: Smooth tissue that cushions joints and covers the ends of bones.
•  Ligaments: Tough bands connecting bone to bone to strengthen joints.
•  Tendons: Fibrous tissue attaching muscles to bones.

Introduction
Tooth decay, also known as dental caries, is the destruction of the tooth’s hard
tissues (enamel and dentine) caused by acid-producing bacteria. It is a
common oral health problem globally and can affect both children and adults.
2. Process of Tooth Decay
The process of tooth decay occurs in stages:
1.  Plaque Formation:
a.  A sticky film called plaque forms on teeth when bacteria in the
mouth mix with saliva and food particles.
b.  Plaque is particularly concentrated in areas that are hard to clean,
like between teeth and along the gumline.
2.  Acid Production:
a.  Bacteria in plaque metabolize sugars and carbohydrates from
food.
b.  This produces acid that attacks the tooth enamel.
3.  Demineralization:
a.  The acids gradually dissolve minerals (calcium and phosphate) in
enamel.
b.  This weakens the enamel, creating tiny holes or cavities.
4.  Progression into Dentine:
a.  If untreated, decay spreads to dentine, the softer layer beneath
enamel.
b.  Dentine is more sensitive and the decay spreads faster.
5.  Pulp Infection:
a.  If decay reaches the pulp (contains nerves and blood vessels), it
causes pain, infection, and possible abscess formation.
b.  This may require root canal treatment or extraction.
Visual cue: Diagram showing enamel → dentine → pulp decay progression.
3. Causes of Tooth Decay
1.  Bacteria in the Mouth:
a.  Mainly Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus species.
b.  These bacteria ferment sugars to produce acids, which erode
enamel.
2.  Sugar Consumption:
a.  Sugary foods and drinks (candies, sodas, pastries) provide food for
bacteria.
b.  Frequent sugar intake increases acid attacks on teeth.
3.  Poor Oral Hygiene:
a.  Inadequate brushing and flossing allow plaque accumulation.
b.  Areas between teeth and along gums are most vulnerable.
4.  Other Factors:
a.  Dry mouth (low saliva reduces neutralization of acids).
b.  Weak enamel (genetic factors).
c.  Acidic foods or drinks.
4. Prevention Methods
A. Brushing
•  Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste.
•  Use a proper technique: 45° angle towards gums, gentle circular
motions.
•  Removes plaque and prevents acid buildup.
B. Flossing
•  Floss once a day to clean between teeth.
•  Prevents decay in interdental spaces, which toothbrushes cannot reach.
C. Reducing Sugar Intake
•  Limit consumption of sugary snacks and drinks.
•  Eat fewer sticky or processed foods, which stay on teeth longer.
•  Substitute with water, fruit, or nuts.
D. Regular Dental Check-ups
•  Visit a dentist every 6–12 months for professional cleaning and early
detection of cavities.
E. Using Antibacterial Mouthwash
•  Reduces plaque bacteria, especially in hard-to-reach areas.
5. Fluoride in Tooth Decay Prevention
A. How Fluoride Works
1.  Strengthens Enamel:
a.  Fluoride replaces lost minerals in enamel, forming fluorapatite,
which is more resistant to acid attacks.
2.  Remineralization:
a.  Helps repair early decay before cavities form.
3.  Inhibits Bacterial Activity:
a.  Reduces acid production by bacteria in plaque.
B. Sources of Fluoride
•  Toothpaste: Most contain 1000–1500 ppm fluoride.
•  Mouth rinses: Used for additional protection.
•  Water fluoridation: Controlled addition of fluoride to public water
supplies.
C. Benefits of Water Fluoridation
•  Reduces dental caries by 20–40% in children and adults.
•  Cost-effective public health measure.
•  Protects populations with limited access to dental care.
D. Controversies and Concerns
1.  Dental Fluorosis:
a.  Caused by excess fluoride during tooth development, leading to
white or brown spots on teeth.
2.  Ethical Concerns:
a.  Some argue that adding fluoride to water removes personal
choice.
3.  Toxicity:
a.  At very high levels (much above recommended), fluoride can be
harmful.
4.  Scientific Consensus:
a.  At recommended levels (0.7–1.0 ppm), fluoride is safe and
effective.

Concept and relevance on biomolecules
Biomolecules are the building blocks of life. They are molecules that
are essential for the structure, function, and regulation of living
organisms. Biomolecules can be divided into two main categories:
macromolecules and small molecules.
Macromolecules are large, complex molecules that are made up of
many smaller units. The four major types of macromolecules are:
Nucleic acids: These are molecules that carry genetic information.
They include DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (ribonucleic
acid).
Proteins: These are molecules that perform a wide variety of functions
in the body. They include enzymes, hormones, and structural proteins.
Carbohydrates: These are molecules that provide energy to the body.
They include sugars, starches, and fibers.
Lipids: These are molecules that provide energy storage and
insulation to the body. They include fats, oils, and waxes.
Small molecules are smaller, simpler molecules that do not have a
biological role in living organisms. They include vitamins, minerals,
and drugs.
The relevance of biomolecules is that they are essential for life. They
perform a wide variety of functions in the body, including carrying
genetic information, catalyzing reactions, providing energy, and
regulating cellular processes. Understanding the structure and
function of biomolecules is important for understanding life at the
molecular level, and has implications for fields such as medicine,
biotechnology, and agriculture.
